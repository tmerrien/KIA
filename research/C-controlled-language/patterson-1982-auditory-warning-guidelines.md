# Patterson (1982): Guidelines for Auditory Warning Systems on Civil Aircraft

## Core claim
The cockpit warnings of the day were too loud, too abrupt, too many and too alike. They did tell the crew about the fault. But they also stopped the crew from thinking and from talking, and crews were then taught to cancel them by reflex. A warning only has to "draw the crew's attention and convey a small amount of information" (p. 4). It can do that with short bursts at a moderate level that leave silence between them. The bursts should have distinct rhythms, their perceived urgency should match the warning's priority, and there should be a small set of them (up to six immediate-action sounds plus two or three "attensons"). Each one is backed by a short key-word voice message.

## Key evidence
- **A small set of warnings works; a large set does not** (Sec. 2.2.2, 4.2; App. B). Twenty naive listeners learned ten real cockpit warnings. The "first four to six warnings are acquired quickly", and learning slows sharply after about seven (pp. 26, 40). Aircraft then had 13 to 16 warnings. Patterson recommends "no more than six immediate-action warning sounds and up to three attensons" (App. C, Sec. 4). This is more generous than ARINC 726, which allowed four plus two.
- **The repeated pattern drives confusion more than the sound itself** (pp. 26-27). Listeners kept confusing warnings that repeated at the same rate (2.5 Hz) even when their spectra were completely different (a two-tone sound and a siren). The guideline that follows: use 5 or more pulses in "a distinctive temporal pattern" (App. C, Sec. 2).
- **Urgency should match priority** (Sec. 2.2.1, 2.2.3, 4). Pulses closer than 150 ms apart sound urgent. Pulses more than 300 ms apart sound non-urgent, like a hostess-call chime. In existing aircraft, some low-priority alerts (passenger evacuation, selective call) sounded *more* urgent than the immediate-action warnings. The design goal is for a warning to "state their priority level in their sound character, so that it is immediately obvious and does not require further cognitive processing" (p. 38).
- **A warning should give its message once and then go quiet** (Sec. 0.3, Figs. 0.1-0.2). In the prototype "undercarriage unsafe" warning, the sound plays at a moderate level, a voice message follows once, and the warning then drops to a background level the crew can talk over. If the fault persists, it comes back in its most urgent form. In each 32-second cycle the information is presented 7 times, yet "the time taken to present the information is less than one quarter of the total" (p. 7). Escalation is tied to the problem continuing. The warning does not start at full volume.
- **Key-word versus full-phrase voice messages** (Sec. 0.3.5, 5.1; App. C, Sec. 5). Immediate-action warnings should use "brief, key-word messages" that are not repeated. Here only a few alternatives are possible and each one is already paired with a learned sound, so the versatility of speech adds little. Abnormal-condition alerts have many alternatives and no unique sound, so they should use "full-phrase format" and be repeated after a pause, relying on "the natural redundancy of language". Patterson also states the cost of the key-word format directly: "The comprehension of key-word messages takes as long as a complete phrase with the same information, and the phrase is more redundant" (p. 10). The only benefit of key words is that they occupy the channel for less time.
- **Speech has costs as a warning** (Sec. 5.1). There is already a lot of speech in the cockpit, so a voice warning has little perceptual contrast with it. A voice message "occupies the entire auditory communication channel... for a relatively long period", and it is hard to talk over a recorded message.

## Relevance to AI answer style
- **The urgency of the delivery should match the importance of the content.** Patterson's central failure case is a low-priority alert that sounds more urgent than an emergency. In text, the equivalents are bold, warning markers, caveats and "IMPORTANT" labels. If they appear on routine points, the reader stops noticing them, and the one real warning is lost. The style of a warning has to signal its priority without the reader having to think about it.
- **Precision versus compression, with evidence.** Patterson approves the key-word format only where the set of messages is small, closed and already learned. There a single word picks out one meaning from a known set: that is precision. Where the set is open, he requires full phrases, because the redundancy of ordinary language does real work. He also reports that key words are *not* understood faster than an equivalent phrase. They only take less time to deliver. Telegraphic style therefore saves the writer's channel time, not the reader's processing time. It is safe only when the terms are already shared.
- **A small set of distinct signals.** If answers use recurring markers (status labels, severity tags, section types), keep the set small (about seven at most) and make each marker differ from the others in form, not only in label.
- **Say it once, then get out of the way, and escalate only if the problem persists.** State a warning once, clearly. Do not repeat it in every paragraph. Raise it again more strongly only if the user's next step shows it was missed.

## Candidate rules for the harness
1. **Match emphasis to priority.** Use warning formatting (bold, "Warning:", "Important") only for items that would cause harm or failure if missed. If more than one or two items in an answer get it, downgrade the rest.
2. **Use key words only for closed sets.** Use terse key-word labels (status words, single-term verdicts) only when the term comes from a small vocabulary the user already shares. For anything open-ended or new to the user, write a full sentence. A shorter message is not understood any faster.
3. **State a caveat once, and escalate only on evidence.** Give each caveat once, where it applies. Restate it more strongly only if later turns show the user has missed or contradicted it.
