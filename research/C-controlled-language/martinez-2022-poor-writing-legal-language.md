## Core claim

Contracts contain far more low-frequency jargon, center-embedded clauses, passives and non-standard capitalization than nine baseline genres of English. In experiments, excerpts with these features are recalled and understood worse. Center-embedding hurts recall most. The authors conclude that the difficulty of legal text comes mainly from working-memory load imposed by long-distance syntactic dependencies (poor writing), not from specialized legal concepts the reader lacks.

## Key evidence

**From the abstract (verified).**
- **Corpus:** about 10 million words. Contracts showed "startlingly high proportions" of four features, measured against nine baseline genres of written and spoken English:
  - low-frequency jargon;
  - center-embedded clauses ("leading to long-distance syntactic dependencies");
  - passive voice;
  - non-standard capitalization.
- **Experiments:** two experiments, N = 184 in total. Excerpts with the features were recalled and comprehended at lower rates "even for experienced readers". Center-embedded clauses "inhibited recall more-so than other features".
- **Three stated conclusions:**
  - (a) The results undermine the "specialized concepts account" of legal theory.
  - (b) The difficulty comes "largely from working-memory limitations imposed by long-distance syntactic dependencies (i.e., poor writing) as opposed to a mere lack of specialized legal knowledge".
  - (c) Editing out the features "would be tractable and beneficial".

**From the MIT news release (verified as the release's wording, not the paper's).**
- **Method:** participants read either the original passage or a version with features altered "without changing meaning".
- **Per-feature results:**
  - Passive voice and non-standard capitalization "did not make the documents harder to understand".
  - Center-embedding was the biggest source of difficulty. Comprehension and recall improved most when a center-embedded definition was pulled out and the term was defined separately.
  - Replacing jargon with common words (lessee/lessor → tenant/landlord) also improved comprehension and recall, but less than removing center-embedding.
- **Quotes:**
  - Gibson: "This is the first time someone has been able to say, here's what makes legal language difficult to understand."
  - Martínez: "We found more words that could have been simplified in legal text than in any other genre that we looked at."

**Discrepancies (UNVERIFIED which is right).**
- **Corpus size:** the release says "about 3.5 million words of legal contracts" compared with other genres. The abstract says n ≈ 10 million words. This may be contracts alone versus the whole corpus.
- **Passives and capitalization:** the abstract groups all four features as hurting performance. The release says passives and capitalization had no significant effect. Only the full text can settle whether those two effects were null.

**From the companion PNAS 2023 paper, which reuses the 2022 materials.** This is per the existing note `martinez-2023-lawyers-legalese-plain-register.md`; I did not re-read it this session.

## Relevance to AI answer style

This is the cleanest empirical split between PRECISION and COMPRESSION in this track. Reader difficulty in an expert register came from *structure*, chiefly center-embedded material that holds a dependency open across a long span. It did not come from the specialized *concepts* themselves.

The authors make two edits:
- **Restructuring:** pull the embedded definition out into its own sentence. This adds words and loses nothing.
- **Term replacement:** use a frequent word when a rare one carries no extra meaning, as with lessee → tenant. This changes no content.

Neither edit is compression, and the gains were largest for de-embedding, which lengthens text. For an assistant this means:
1. Do not shorten an answer by nesting qualifications inside the main clause. A flat sequence of short clauses is easier on the reader even when it is longer.
2. Keep the exact technical term when it carries the idea. Replace it only when a common synonym is truly equivalent.
3. Passive voice is a low-priority target. The release says it made no significant difference; the abstract's grouping leaves this UNVERIFIED.

Expertise did not protect readers ("even for experienced readers"; see also PNAS 2023, which found no register × training interaction). So "the user is an expert" does not license dense nesting.

## Candidate rules for the harness

1. **No center-embedding.** Never put a definition, condition or exception inside the clause it modifies, between subject and verb. State the main claim first, then add the qualifier as its own sentence or bullet. Accept the extra words.
2. **Swap only the word, not the meaning.** Replace a low-frequency word with a common one only when the two mean exactly the same thing. Keep the specialist term when it is the precise one, and define it once in a separate clause rather than inline.
3. **Expert audience is no excuse.** A technical reader does not justify long-distance dependencies. Simplify syntax for experts too, and keep their vocabulary.
