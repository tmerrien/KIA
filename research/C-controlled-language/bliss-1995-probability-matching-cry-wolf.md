# Bliss, Gilson & Deaton (1995): Human probability matching in response to alarms of varying reliability

## Core claim
People do not respond to every alarm. Instead, the proportion of alarms they respond to matches the probability that an alarm is true. This is **probability matching**, and it is the measurable form of the "cry-wolf" effect. A false alarm costs more than the moment it wastes: it lowers the response rate to every later alarm from the same source, true alarms included.

## Key evidence
All from the PubMed abstract unless noted.
- **Design.** There were 138 undergraduates, each doing two blocks of a cognitively demanding psychomotor primary task. Alarms were 25%, 50% or 75% true. Urgency varied: green, yellow or red visual alarms, each paired with a low-, medium- or high-urgency civil aircraft cockpit auditory alarm. The measures were response frequency, speed and accuracy, analysed with t-tests and repeated-measures MANOVAs.
- **Main result.** "Most subjects (about 90%) do not respond to all alarms but match their response rates to the expected probability of true alarms (probability matching)."
- **Minority strategy.** "About 10% of the subjects responded in the extreme, utilizing an all-or-none strategy." These people either always responded or never responded.
- **Urgency and workload are secondary.** In the dissertation abstract, urgency and task difficulty had modest but significant effects. Reliability was the main driver. Louder or redder signals did not undo the reliability effect. The size of these effects in the 1995 paper is UNVERIFIED.
- **How the result is retold.** Edworthy (2013, JAMIA; covered in this track) paraphrases it as "if the accuracy rate of an alarm is 90% then people will respond slightly more than 90% of the time". The 1995 study tested only 25/50/75%, so the 90% and 10% figures in that paraphrase are an extrapolation, not data from this paper. Cite the 25/50/75% design.
- **Probability matching is not optimal.** In the probability-learning literature it is the textbook suboptimal strategy. When missing a true alarm is costly, the best policy is to respond to every alarm, and an operator who matches will miss about (1 - p) of the true events. Whether the paper frames the result in terms of costs and payoffs is UNVERIFIED (full text not read).

## Relevance to AI answer style
- **Hedges and caveats are alarms.** Every "consult a professional", "results may vary" or "be careful" that does not apply to the case is a false alarm. This study predicts that readers will learn the hit rate of the assistant's caveats and attend to them at about that rate. Boilerplate warnings do more than waste words. They lower attention to the real warning that comes later, so the cost is **lost precision on the one caveat that matters**, not just extra length.
- **Making the warning more urgent does not fix low reliability.** Urgency (red, high-urgency sound) had only modest effects compared with reliability. Bold type, "IMPORTANT:" or capitals on a caveat cannot restore trust that frequent false caveats have spent. Only a higher hit rate can.
- **Precision, not compression.** The fix is not to delete warnings. That is compression, and it drops the true alarm. The fix is to make each warning specific and conditional: name the hazard, the condition under which it applies, and the action. A specific warning is checkable, so readers can calibrate it as reliable. A generic one is unfalsifiable, so they calibrate it as noise.
- **Some readers ignore everything; some read everything.** The 10% all-or-none minority means some readers will skip every caveat. This argues for putting the critical warning where a skimmer cannot miss it (first line, or attached to the risky step) rather than in a closing block of disclaimers.

## Candidate rules for the harness
1. **Warn only on a live trigger.** Add a caveat only when it applies to this user's stated case and would change what they do. Drop generic disclaimers ("consult a professional", "results may vary") unless the case actually calls for one.
2. **Make every caveat specific.** State the hazard, the condition and the action in one sentence (for example, "If the table has over 10M rows, this lock blocks writes; run it off-peak"). Never use a bare "be careful".
3. **Do not use emphasis to stand in for reliability.** Use at most one highlighted warning per answer and place it at the risky step. Keep high-salience markers (bold, "Warning:") for consequences that are irreversible or cause harm, so the marker keeps its predictive value.
