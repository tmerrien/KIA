## Core claim

Contracts were redrafted in three cumulative stages:
1. remove the archaic and redundant terms;
2. simplify the words and the sentence structure;
3. define the legal terms, or replace them with simpler ones.

The second stage gave the reliable gain in comprehension. Even so, absolute comprehension "were still very low". The errors show that non-experts struggle with legal *concepts* that "sometimes conflict with prior knowledge and beliefs". Simpler language does not remove that difficulty.

## Key evidence

- **Design (abstract).** A set of standard legal contracts was redrafted in three cumulative stages, which gave three modified versions plus the original. Comprehension was measured with **paraphrasing** and **question-answering** tasks given to naive readers.
- **Result (abstract).** "Comprehension ... was reliably enhanced by the use of simplified words and sentence structure, but absolute levels of comprehension were still very low."
  - The abstract does not credit a reliable gain to stage 1 (archaic and redundant terms) or to stage 3 (defining or replacing legal terms). It names only stage 2. Whether stages 1 and 3 had no reliable effect is UNVERIFIED without the full text.
- **Effect sizes (via Kimble, citing pp. 75 and 77).**
  - In one test, comprehension rose from 15% to 36%, a 140% relative gain.
  - In another test, it rose from 50.5% to 66%, a 31% relative gain.
  - Which tasks and which version pairs these numbers compare is UNVERIFIED.
- **Error analysis (abstract).** Erroneous responses showed difficulty with complex legal concepts "quite apart from the constraints of language". Some of those concepts conflict with readers' prior beliefs about how the law works.
- **Participants.** One search snippet reported 24 clerical staff and 24 age-matched students reading four contracts of about 400 words each. It attributed this to an eScholarship replication (qt5k09w2td), which could not be opened. **UNVERIFIED.**

## Relevance to AI answer style

This is the key empirical case for the difference between **precision** and **compression**.
- **What the redrafting showed.** Plain syntax and familiar words (stage 2) made the text easier to process. That is the legitimate gain: the reader spends less effort on the form of the text.
- **What it did not show.** Defining or replacing the legal term (stage 3) is not credited with a reliable gain. Comprehension stayed "very low" because the difficulty sat in the concept itself. Examples are a doctrine such as indemnity, or a remedy that runs against a lay reader's intuitions.
- **What this means for terms.**
  - Swapping a precise term for a vaguer everyday word does not deliver the concept. It can hide the fact that there was a concept to understand.
  - Deleting the term to save words is compression. Meaning is lost and nothing is gained.
- **What this means for the assistant.**
  - When an answer depends on a technical concept, the assistant should keep the exact term.
  - It should add the *one* clause that maps the concept onto the reader's prior beliefs, especially where the concept contradicts them. A shorter synonym does not do this.
  - Simplifying the sentence is not the same as being understood. Where the concept is counterintuitive, say so plainly. Examples: "a landlord can still...", "unlike what you might expect...".
- **Caution.** Martínez et al. (2022) found the opposite weighting for processing difficulty: poor writing mattered more than specialised terms. Reconciling the two:
  - fix the syntax first, as both studies agree;
  - concept-level misunderstanding is a separate failure that syntax fixes do not reach.

## Candidate rules for the harness

1. **Simplify the syntax before you touch the terms.** Break up long sentences and nested clauses, and replace archaic or doubled wording. Keep the precise term that carries the idea. Do not trade it for a vaguer everyday word.
2. **Name where the concept defies expectation.** When a key concept conflicts with what a non-expert would assume, give the term plus one clause that states the conflict. Examples: "X means Y, not Z as you might expect", or a short example. Do not assume that plain wording alone makes the idea understood.
3. **Do not equate readability with comprehension.** For high-stakes content (legal, medical, financial), offer a quick check that tests the reader's understanding. Examples: a concrete scenario, or "in practice this means...". Do not stop at shortening.
