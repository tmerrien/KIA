## Core claim

Writing airplane-maintenance procedures in AECMA Simplified English (SE) significantly improves readers' comprehension, but only for the more complex procedure. For that procedure it also helps readers find where information sits in the document, and it costs no extra reading or completion time. The benefit appears larger for non-native English readers, although the authors could not test this statistically.

## Key evidence

- **Design (abstract):** a factorial comparison of document type (SE vs. non-SE), passage (Procedure A vs. Procedure B), and native language (native vs. non-native English speakers). The measures were comprehension score, content-location score (identifying where information appears), and task completion time.
- **Materials (Roturier 2006, p. 147):** the documents were genuine SE and non-SE versions, not texts written for the experiment. There were two documents, each in two versions of the same procedure.
- **Subjects (Roturier 2006, p. 147):** 121 undergraduate students rather than maintenance technicians. The exact split between natives and non-natives is UNVERIFIED. The abstract says only that cell sizes were "extremely different".
- **Results (abstract):**
  - SE significantly improved comprehension of the *more complex* document.
  - Readers of the more complex SE document located and identified information more easily.
  - SE and non-SE versions took "essentially the same amount of time" to read and complete the test.
  - Non-native readers appeared to benefit from SE more than natives. This was not tested statistically.
- **Boundary (Roturier 2006, ch. 6, citing the IPCC version, p. 173):** the authors had assumed the two procedures were comparable. When the results differed, they re-examined the complexity of the source materials and concluded that the effect was limited to one procedure. The gain is therefore conditional on how complex the text is. It is not a general effect of SE.
- **The authors' own caveat (Holmback, Shubert & Spyridakis 1996, CLAW 96, p. 176, as quoted by Roturier 2006):** "taking a comprehension test is probably not the best way to measure comprehension of a procedure (...) since procedures are written to be performed, not quizzed". The study measures understanding, not whether readers perform the task correctly.
- Effect sizes, means and p-values are UNVERIFIED because the full text was not read.

## Relevance to AI answer style

- **Precision vs. compression.** The study backs SE as a *precision* discipline: one word per meaning, explicit action statements, and controlled syntax. It does not back shortening. SE versions took about the same time to read. The gain came from removing ambiguity, not from cutting words. Wang & Friginal (2026) sharpen the point: STE's shorter texts made reading faster, but they did not make each word easier to process.
- **The gain depends on complexity.** SE helped only the more complex procedure. For an assistant, strict controlled phrasing pays off on multi-step, multi-condition answers (procedures, configuration steps, troubleshooting). Imposing it on simple answers buys nothing.
- **Findability is a separate benefit.** Readers of the complex SE text located information more easily. Consistent terms and one-action steps make an answer scannable, which links to the F-shaped reading and information-scent work in other tracks.
- **Non-native readers.** The gain looked larger for non-native readers but was not tested statistically. Weight it as a hint, not a rule.

## Candidate rules for the harness

1. **For multi-step or conditional procedures, write one action per step, start each step with an explicit imperative verb, and use the same term for the same thing throughout.** Never switch synonyms ("config file", "settings file", "the YAML") for one object. This is a precision rule: it removes ambiguity without dropping content.
2. **Do not shorten a procedure by dropping conditions, warnings or object names.** SE's benefit came at equal reading time, so clarity, not length, is what improves comprehension. If a step depends on a condition, state the condition inside that step.
3. **Scale the strictness to complexity.** A one-line factual answer needs no procedural formatting. The controlled-language benefit appeared only for the complex document.
