# Morrow & Prinzo (1999): grouped vs. digit-by-digit numbers, and message length, in GA pilot communication

Note: the queued work (Morrow & Rodvold 1993, NASA CR-177621) already has a note (`morrow-1993-message-length-timing.md`). To avoid repeating it, this note covers its direct follow-up by the same first author.

## Citation
Morrow, D. G., & Prinzo, O. V. (1999, July). *Improving Pilot/ATC Voice Communication in General Aviation*. Final Report DOT/FAA/AM-99/21. FAA Office of Aviation Medicine, Washington, DC (Civil Aeromedical Institute, Oklahoma City; University of New Hampshire). Contract DTFA-02-96-P-54069, Task AM-B-96-HRR-513. 27 pp.
Open access: https://www.faa.gov/sites/faa.gov/files/data_research/research/med_humanfacs/oamtechreports/AM99-21.pdf (I read the full report, including the method, all results sections, Tables 1-12, the discussion, and the references). There is also a ROSA P record: https://rosap.ntl.bts.gov/view/dot/57906

## Core claim
Message length had a clear effect. As ATC messages grew from 2 to 5 speech acts, General Aviation pilots made more readback errors and asked for more clarifications, and these costs grew with age and with lower digit span, both signs of working-memory load. Saying numbers in grouped form ("forty-one hundred", "one twenty-three point sixty-five") instead of digit by digit ("four thousand one hundred", "one two three point six five") helped only a little: it reduced clarification requests, and only in the balanced subset of 3- vs 5-act messages, where the gain was largest for the long messages. Pilots trained on the familiar digit-by-digit form often translated grouped numbers back into it when reading back, so a new format, even a more compact one, pays a transition cost.

## Key evidence
- Design: 24 GA pilots (mean 1311 flight hours; 21 instrument-rated) flew two 1-hour missions around Oklahoma City in the CAMI BGARS simulator. They heard 29 recorded ATC messages per mission and read back every instruction from memory, with no note-taking allowed. Between subjects: Grouping (altitudes and frequencies always grouped, everything else digit by digit) vs Control (everything digit by digit). Within subjects: message length (2-5 speech acts including call sign) and instruction type (critical altitude/frequency vs other). Age and WAIS-R digit span were covariates.
- Length effect, all messages (Table 6, mean proportions for 2/3/4/5 speech acts): readback errors .00/.03/.06/.07 (F(3,126)=3.2, p<.05; quadratic, levelling off at 4-5); requests for clarification .01/.03/.08/.17 (F(3,126)=3.9, p<.05). The table text is garbled by OCR, but these two columns agree with Tables 8 and 9. Readback order mismatches (pilots reordering instructions) also rose linearly with length. The authors read this as a coping strategy, possibly a recency effect.
- Thresholds: the jump comes between 3 and 4 speech acts, that is, between a call sign plus 2 instructions and a call sign plus 3. Requests more than doubled again at 5 acts.
- Grouping effect: no effect on readback errors in any analysis. Fewer requests for clarification only in the balanced 3- vs 5-act critical-message analysis (Grouping .07 vs Control .13; F(1,42)=4.1, p<.05). The gain interacted with length (3 acts: .02 vs .04; 5 acts: .14 vs .19; F(1,42)=5.6, p<.05). The benefit spread to non-grouped instructions in the same message (no condition x type interaction). The authors explain this as *unique encoding*: when one class of numbers is coded differently, it interferes less with the others. Loftus et al. (1979) and Burki-Cohen (1995) report the same pattern.
- Format reversion: Grouping pilots changed the format in their readback far more often (critical instructions .15 vs .07; other instructions .01 vs .00), mostly turning grouped numbers back into digit-by-digit form, despite 20-25 minutes of training on grouped numbers. Pilots with higher digit span reverted more, which suggests the translation costs resources.
- Individual differences: higher digit span went with fewer clarification requests (r=-.45) and fewer reorderings. Older pilots (median split at 26) made more requests on 5-act messages only (.22 vs .12), an age x complexity interaction.
- Caveats stated by the authors: small heterogeneous sample, few readback errors (floor effect), groups unbalanced on age despite random assignment, and earlier grouping studies (Parker-Haney 1991; Burki-Cohen 1995) found no benefit or even harm for long messages. They do not recommend changing the ATC format.

## Relevance to AI answer style
- **Precision vs compression, made concrete.** Grouped numbers are *shorter* to say ("thirty-five hundred" vs "three thousand five hundred") but carry the same value. This is compression without meaning loss, and it still gave little benefit. It cost a translation step whenever it broke the listener's habitual format. A shorter form helps only if it is the form the reader already uses. For an assistant, use the reader's canonical notation (the exact version string, the CLI flag as typed, the unit the user used) rather than a terser paraphrase or abbreviation.
- **Load per unit, not total length, drives errors.** Errors and repeat requests jumped once a message carried 3 or more instructions. This matches Morrow & Rodvold (1993) and Prinzo (2009): about two actions per chunk is the safe zone, and beyond that readers drop or reorder items.
- **Distinct coding reduces interference.** Making one class of values look different (grouped altitudes vs digit-by-digit headings) helped memory for the *whole* message. For written answers, give similar-looking values different labels, formatting, or lines (code formatting for paths and commands, units always attached) so they do not intrude on each other.
- **Readers reorder long lists.** Reordering rose with length. If order matters (steps), number the steps. Do not rely on prose sequence inside one long sentence.
- **Weaker readers pay most for long messages.** The length cost was largest for older and lower-span pilots. Keep units small by default, because the cost of over-packing falls on the reader least able to bear it.

## Candidate rules for the harness
1. **Use the reader's canonical form for exact values.** Repeat identifiers, numbers, versions, and commands in exactly the notation the user or tool uses. Do not shorten or restyle them to save characters, because a reformatted value forces a translation step and invites errors.
2. **Cap instructions per sentence at two.** If a sentence would carry three or more actions or values, split it into numbered steps so the order is explicit and items cannot be dropped or reordered.
3. **Make co-occurring value types visually distinct.** When one answer mixes kinds of values (ports and versions, paths and flags, durations and counts), format each kind consistently and differently (labels, units, code spans) so they do not interfere.
