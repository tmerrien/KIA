## Core claim
An alarm works only if three layers are right. The first is the trigger (signal detection). The second is the alarm philosophy: what gets an alarm, at what priority, and in which modality. Only then does the sound design matter. Because false-alarm rates are extremely high, people learn to discount alarms, and this wipes out the benefit of good sound design. Within the design layer, the review gives these rules: keep alarms to a minimum, map perceived urgency to the hazard, and make the alarms in a set as *different from one another* as possible. The IEC 60601-1-8 melodies break the last rule because they are uniform.

## Key evidence
- **False alarms swamp the signal.** One survey found that "of 1455 soundings of alarms, only eight were associated with potentially life-threatening problems" (O'Carroll 1986). For cardiopulmonary monitors, false-alarm rates of 85-99% are reported (Talley et al. 2011).
- **Response probability tracks perceived reliability.** In Bliss, Gilson & Deaton (1995), "if the accuracy rate of an alarm is 90% then people will respond slightly more than 90% of the time". At 10% accuracy, response rates fall to just above 10%. People ration their attention to a signal according to how often it has been right in the past.
- **The trigger comes before the sound.** "The best designed alarm in the world will not work unless it is supported by good signal detection and interpretation." Adding delays before an alarm fires reduces ignored alarms more than it reduces effective ones (Gorges et al. 2009). Reduction case by case (Keller et al. 2011) brought alarm counts down to very low levels.
- **Alarm philosophy.** "Alarms should be kept to a minimum"; "proliferation of alarms is never a good idea". More alarms take longer to learn, cause more confusion, and push volumes up. Use one-to-one alarm-to-function mapping for high-priority situations and priority coding for the rest. Match hazard to urgency, choose the modality to suit the content and the setting, and standardise.
- **Urgency mapping is a solved design problem, but it is often not applied.** The work on acoustic parameters and perceived urgency (Edworthy, Loxley & Dennis 1991; Hellier, Edworthy & Dennis 1993; Haas & Edworthy 1996) could "readily lead to a properly urgency-mapped 'master' alarm varying only in its urgency". Mismatches between how urgent an alarm sounds and how urgent the situation is are frequently reported.
- **Uniformity causes confusion.** "The alarms currently specified in IEC 60601-1-8 each possess the same number of pulses and are uniform in rhythm." Melodies whose contours are shared are readily confused and hard to learn (Sanderson, Wee & Lacherez 2006; Wee & Sanderson 2008). Overlapping alarms are "almost indiscriminable" (Lacherez, Seah & Sanderson 2007). Heterogeneous sets are easier to learn (Edworthy, Hellier, Titchener et al. 2011).
- **Meaning-bearing sounds win on learnability.** Auditory icons (sounds whose source maps to the meaning, such as a heartbeat) and speech are the easiest to learn. With speech there is "little scope for misunderstanding". The costs are acceptability (icons do not sound like alarms) and context: speech "might be of little use in a noisy hospital environment" but "might be used to great effect in a quieter, home environment".
- **Conclusion.** "Alarms that are shrill and irritating do not constitute good design." Standards must rest on evidence, and "It is not clear that this is the case for IEC 60601-1-8."

## Relevance to AI answer style
- **Precision versus compression in signal sets.** The IEC melodies are compressed: short, abstract and uniform, so they save channel time and lose discriminability. Auditory icons and speech are precise: each signal carries its own meaning, so it can be recognised without a learned code. If an assistant uses a small vocabulary of recurring markers (verdicts, status words, severity tags), the markers should differ in form and should mean something on their own. An arbitrary code that only works once it has been learned fails this test.
- **The "false alarm" problem applies to hedges and warnings.** If 99% of caveats are boilerplate, the probability-matching result predicts that readers will ignore about 99% of them, including the one that matters. A caveat earns attention only if past caveats were reliable, so the trigger threshold (when to warn at all) matters more than how the warning is worded.
- **One alarm per critical function; priority coding for the rest.** Critical items each get their own explicit, named statement. Minor items can share a single, lower-key "notes" channel.
- **Modality and context.** Speech is best in a quiet channel and worst in a noisy one. In a dense answer, a warning set in prose among other prose has little contrast. It needs its own position (first, or set apart) to be noticed.

## Candidate rules for the harness
1. **Warn only above threshold.** Add a caveat or warning only if missing it would plausibly cause harm, failure or a wrong decision in this user's situation. Do not add generic hedges. Every unneeded warning lowers the response rate to the next one.
2. **Make recurring labels self-describing and mutually distinct.** Status and severity labels should come from a small set, and each should carry its meaning as a plain word ("Blocked", "Works", "Untested"). Avoid near-identical codes or symbols the reader must learn.
3. **Give each critical point its own statement and pool the rest.** State each must-act item separately and explicitly. Group the minor notes under one low-emphasis heading, rather than giving every point equal salience.
