# Gibson (1998): Linguistic complexity: Locality of syntactic dependencies

**Citation.** Gibson, E. (1998). Linguistic complexity: Locality of syntactic dependencies. *Cognition*, 68(1), 1-76. doi:10.1016/S0010-0277(98)00034-1
**Full text read:** https://tedlab.mit.edu/tedlab_website/researchpapers/Gibson_1998_Cogn.pdf (author's lab copy of the Elsevier PDF, all 76 pages; metadata cross-checked on Crossref, which counted 1,520 citing works in Oct 2026).
**Name note.** The 1998 paper calls the theory the *Syntactic Prediction Locality Theory* (SPLT). Gibson's later work renames it the *Dependency Locality Theory* (DLT).

## Core claim
Processing difficulty comes from two locality costs, not from sentence length as such. (1) *Integration cost*: attaching a word to its head or dependent costs more the more new discourse referents (new noun phrases and tensed verbs) lie between them. (2) *Memory cost*: holding an open syntactic prediction (for example "a verb must still come") costs more the longer it stays open. Comprehension breaks down at about 2M(3) memory units: two long open predictions at once, each spanning about three new referents (p. 41).

## Key evidence
- **Cost formula (p. 12-13, def. 9).** Integration cost = the cost of building the new structure + I(n), where n is the number of *new discourse referents* processed since the head was last active. As a first approximation the paper sets I(n) = n. Words that add no new referent add little distance cost. Gibson justifies this with Haviland & Clark (1974): building a new referent is expensive.
- **Relative clauses (p. 20-24, Figs. 3-4).** Object-extracted clauses ("The reporter who the senator attacked admitted the error") are harder than subject-extracted ones ("...who attacked the senator..."), even though both have the same words. The predicted costs track word-by-word reading times in King & Just (1991). They also track Gibson & Ko's self-paced reading data (48 participants, 91% comprehension accuracy). Reading slows exactly at the verbs where long integrations happen.
- **Given vs. new referents (p. 17-19, Fig. 2; Gibson & Warren 1998a).** Doubly nested clauses become acceptable when the innermost subject is "I" or "you", referents that are already in the discourse: "The student who the professor who I collaborated with had advised copied the article." The same sentence with "Jen", "the scientist", or an unanchored "they" is rated harder. So *what* sits between dependents matters, not how many words.
- **Heaviness / end-weight (sec. 4, p. 51-53).** "talked [to the visitor who had just arrived and was staying for the summer] [about the problem]" costs I(4) at "about". The reordered "talked [about the problem] [to the visitor ...]" costs I(0)/I(1). The paper also gives a production account: speakers put heavy phrases last so they do not have to hold a pending head in mind. Extraposition works the same way. "It bothered the intern that the administrator ... had lost the reports" costs I(1), while the sentential-subject version costs I(5) (ex. 57).
- **Scope.** The same metric also covers center-embedding overload in English and Japanese, the advantage of cross-serial over nested dependencies (Dutch vs. German), and several ambiguity-resolution preferences (sec. 7 summary list).
- **Caveat the author states.** I(n) = n is a simplification; the cost functions are non-linear at large n (sec. 2.6.7). Section 6 also notes that it is theoretically awkward for *both* costs to grow with distance under an activation account.

## Relevance to AI answer style (precision vs. compression)
- **Length is the wrong target.** DLT gives a mechanism for the distinction this project draws. A short sentence that separates a subject from its verb with a nested clause costs more than a longer sentence whose dependencies are adjacent. Cutting words does not reduce reading cost unless it shortens head-dependent distances. *Compression* can even raise cost: dropping relative pronouns and function words packs more new referents between dependents.
- **What counts as distance is new referents.** Intervening material costs little when it refers to things already in the discourse ("I", "you", terms already defined). So an answer that introduces a precise term once and then reuses that term is cheaper than one that adds fresh paraphrases and synonyms. This is a processing argument for *precision* through consistent terminology. It matches the ASD-STE100 "one word, one meaning" rule.
- **End-weight.** Put the short, load-bearing element (the verdict, the command, the value) first, and put the long qualifier or list last. Clausal subjects should be extraposed ("It matters that X ..." rather than "That X ... matters"). This agrees with Gopen & Swan's stress-position advice, but for a different reason: it frees working memory rather than serving emphasis.
- **Limit.** The evidence comes from constructed English sentences read by native speakers, mostly from self-paced reading data. The paper does not test whole answers, lists, or code. Applying it to L2 readers (as Wang & Friginal 2026 do) is an extrapolation.

## Candidate rules for the harness
1. **Keep dependents adjacent.** Do not put more than one new entity (a noun phrase or clause) between a subject and its verb, or between a verb and its required object or complement. Move long modifiers after the head they modify, or into a separate sentence.
2. **Heavy last.** When a verb takes a short and a long complement, put the short one first. Extrapose clausal subjects: write "It fails when the cache is cold and the index is rebuilding", not "That the cache is cold and ... causes failure".
3. **Reuse, don't re-introduce.** After naming a term, refer back with that same term or a pronoun. Do not use a new synonym that the reader must treat as a new referent. Measure concision by reading cost (the distances between dependent words), not by word count.
