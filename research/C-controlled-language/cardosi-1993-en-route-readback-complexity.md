# Cardosi (1993): An Analysis of En Route Controller-Pilot Voice Communications

## Citation

Cardosi, K. M. (1993). *An Analysis of En Route Controller-Pilot Voice Communications*. Final Report, March 1993 (reprint July 1993). DOT/FAA/RD-93/11 and DOT-VNTSC-FAA-93-2. Cambridge, MA: John A. Volpe National Transportation Systems Center, for the FAA Research and Development Service (ARD-210, Human Performance Program). NTIS. DTIC accession ADA264784 (UNVERIFIED; this number comes from a BibBase record only).

Full text (30-page scan) read: https://rosap.ntl.bts.gov/view/dot/8659/dot_8659_DS1.pdf (record: https://rosap.ntl.bts.gov/view/dot/8659)

## Core claim

Unexpected radio clearances that pack in more separate pieces of information go wrong more often. A clearance with five or more elements is about three times as likely to need a repeat as one with one to four elements (8% vs 1-3%). Cardosi concludes that "two shorter transmissions may be more effectively transmitted than one longer one". She recommends that unexpected en route transmissions "contain no more than four pieces of information".

## Key evidence

- **Corpus.** 47 hours of real ATC tape from 8 ARTCCs (Atlanta, Chicago, Kansas City, Los Angeles, New York, Oakland, Seattle, Salt Lake), 6 hours each, but only 5 hours from Seattle because of a faulty tape. The sample has 24 high-workload and 23 moderate-workload hours. It holds 5,032 controller-to-pilot transmissions, of which 3,576 are clearances. Four analysts did the coding: one former controller and three pilots. The abstract says "forty-eight hours"; the body says 47.
- **Unit of complexity (the "element").** An element is "each word, or set of words... that contained a new piece of information to the pilot, and was critical to the understanding of the message". It is also "an opportunity for error". The call sign is not counted, because it only attracts attention and does not have to be remembered. A direction counts only when it is a separate chance to err. "Turn left heading 090" is 2 elements. "Climb and maintain 12,000" from 10,000 ft is 1, because climb is implied. "Cross ten miles west of Pullman at and maintain FL200" is 4. Cardosi admits that "any such counting scheme is necessarily arbitrary" and that elements do not all impose the same memory load.
- **Distribution (Table 1).** 48% of clearances had 1 element, 30% had 2, 11% had 3, 6% had 4 and 5% had 5 or more. Most real messages are already short.
- **Acknowledgement types (Table 2).** Full readback 71%, partial readback 12%, "roger" only 6%, other replies 8%, no acknowledgement 3%. Under 1% of full readbacks contained an error, against 2% of partial readbacks.
- **Readback errors against complexity (Fig. 1).** There were 27 errors in total. At 1 element: 7 errors in 1,733 clearances, which is 0.4%. The rate doubles from 0.7% at 3 elements to 1.4% at 4. Clearances with 5 or more elements are 4% of all clearances but carry 26% of the errors.
- **Full readbacks collapse as messages grow (Table 3).** Among the erroneous readbacks, about 66% were full readbacks at 4 elements, but only 20% at 5 elements and 0% at 6. Long messages are acknowledged in part, and that removes the hearback check.
- **Requests for repeat (Fig. 2).** 51 requests, which is 1.4% of clearances. The rate is 1-2.5% for 1-4 elements and almost 4% for 5 or more.
- **Combined miscommunication (Fig. 3).** Readback errors plus repeat requests: 1-3% for 1-4 elements and 8% for 5 or more, chi-square(1) = 7.88, p = .005. This is the report's only inferential test.
- **Self-corrections add load.** "Even the simple 'correction' may add to the mental processing load of the pilot by lengthening the entire message", and a correction that replaces information adds more. The tape examples show pilots answering long, self-corrected clearances with partial or confused readbacks ("you want us to cross what?").
- **Recency in hearback.** In one missed hearback error, the controller caught the pilot's altimeter error, which was "the last thing he heard", but missed the speed error. Controllers missed only 3 readback errors (11%). There are too few cases to draw conclusions.
- **Type of information (Table 4).** Radio frequencies caused 41% of readback errors (the executive summary says 37%, an inconsistency inside the report). 7 of the 11 frequency errors involved a change to a new facility. Crossing restrictions caused 18%, and those averaged 5 elements each.
- **Null results.** Similar call signs, weather, blocked transmissions, non-standard phraseology, fast speech and accent each co-occurred with only 1-3 errors. There were too few errors to test any of them.
- **Call-sign drift.** In 29 cases (0.8% of clearances) the pilot used a different call sign from the controller. Only 39% of these were corrected.

Limitations: there were only 27 errors. The study is observational, so it cannot separate message length from controller choice, and complex clearances may cluster in busy periods. Each sector was sampled for one hour, so the analysis did not follow flights or see what a missed partial readback led to. Cardosi says the four-element limit applies to *unexpected* transmissions: "different results would be expected when pilots are prepared for a longer transmission, as in clearance delivery."

## Relevance to AI answer style

- **The element is a precision unit, not a word count.** Cardosi counts only the items that the listener must remember and act on, where each one is a chance to err. Call signs and words like "correction" are not counted, although they still lengthen the message. This is the PRECISION-vs-COMPRESSION distinction in operational form. The cost of a message is the number of independent load-bearing items, not its length. Dropping an element to save words would be COMPRESSION, and it is not what she recommends. She recommends splitting: the same elements, sent in two transmissions. Information is kept and packaging changes.
- **A threshold, not a slope.** Error rates are low and flat up to about 4 elements, then jump at 5 or more. For an assistant, this suggests a soft cap: about four new action items per block before the answer should break into separate steps or ask for confirmation.
- **Partial uptake hides errors.** Long messages get partial readbacks, which remove the check that would have caught a mistake. If an answer puts many instructions in one paragraph, the user may act on the first and last and drop the middle.
- **Corrections cost.** Self-repair inside a message ("X, correction, Y") adds load even when the correction is right. An assistant should state the final value once and not narrate its own revisions inline.
- **Expectation matters.** The cap applies to unexpected messages. When the user has asked for a long procedure (the "clearance delivery" case), a longer, structured list is fine.

## Candidate rules for the harness

1. **Count load-bearing elements, not words.** An element is any value, direction, condition or instruction the user must remember to act correctly. Put no more than about 4 new elements in one unsplit paragraph or sentence chain. If there are more, break them into numbered steps or separate blocks, and keep every element. Never drop one to meet the cap.
2. **State the corrected value once.** Do not write "do A, actually B" inside an instruction. Revise before sending and give only B. If a correction to an earlier turn is needed, put it in its own short opening line.
3. **Fit the cap to expectation.** A short cap applies to unrequested or side information. When the user has asked for a full procedure, a longer numbered list is fine, but each step should still hold no more than about 4 elements.
