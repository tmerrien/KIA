## Core claim

Readers with little knowledge of a domain understand a text better when its links are made explicit: clear referents, connectives, headers and added explanation. Readers with high knowledge learn more deeply from a *minimally* coherent version of the same text, because the gaps make them infer the unstated relations from what they already know. This "reverse cohesion effect" shows up only on measures of situation-model understanding (inference questions, problem solving, key-word sorting). It does not show up on text-base measures (free recall, text-based questions).

## Key evidence

All of the following comes from the abstract, verbatim content.

- The two experiments were motivated by Kintsch's (1988) construction-integration model. Participants were junior-high students reading science (biology) texts.
- **Experiment 1** compared three versions of a biology text, using free recall, written questions and a key-word sorting task. Result: "advantages for globally coherent text and for more explanatory text."
- **Experiment 2** compared four versions that varied *local* and *global* coherence orthogonally, and crossed them with readers' background knowledge. Result: "readers who know little about the domain of the text benefit from a coherent text, whereas high-knowledge readers benefit from a minimally coherent text."
- **The authors' explanation:** "the poorly written text forces the knowledgeable readers to engage in compensatory processing to infer unstated relations in the text."
- **Which measure matters:** free recall and text-based questions tap the *text base*. Inference questions, problem-solving questions and sorting tap the *situation model*. "The rewards to be gained from active processing are primarily at the level of the situation model rather than at the superficial level of text-base understanding."

How cohesion was manipulated, as McNamara et al. (2011, pp. 232-233) describe the 1996 paradigm and its successors:

- High-cohesion versions were made by "adding surface-level indicators of relations between ideas". These ranged from "identifying anaphoric referents, synonymous terms, connective ties, or headers, to supplying background information left unstated in the text."
- The 2011 study lists seven concrete operations, citing McNamara et al. (1996) as a source of methods:
  1. replacing pronouns with noun phrases
  2. adding descriptive elaborations
  3. adding sentence connectives
  4. replacing or inserting words to increase conceptual overlap
  5. adding topic headers
  6. adding theme sentences
  7. reordering sentences into temporal or referential order
- The first author stresses that gap-filling "can be successful only if readers have the sufficient amount of background knowledge that can be accessed or triggered based on limited textual information."

## Relevance to AI answer style

- **Precision versus compression.** Cohesion devices fall into two kinds, and the difference is the core of this question:
  - *Referential* devices restate the noun instead of "it", keep terms consistent and repeat the key word. They cost a few words and remove ambiguity, so they are precision, not padding.
  - *Explanatory* devices supply unstated background and bridging causal steps. They are what an expert can do without.
  - Dropping the referential kind to save words is harmful compression for every reader. Dropping the explanatory kind is legitimate only when the reader can reconstruct it.
- **Calibrate to the reader.** One answer cannot be optimal for both novice and expert. For a novice, terse "expert" prose is a real comprehension failure. For an expert, over-linked prose is redundant at best. The finding that high-knowledge readers *learn more* from gaps is about learning in a study setting. For an assistant whose goal is fast, accurate uptake rather than training, the practical lesson is narrower: experts do not need the bridges, so omitting them costs an expert nothing at the level of understanding. (This is an inference, not tested by the paper.)
- **Measure the right level.** A terse answer can preserve every fact, which is text-base fidelity, and still fail to give a novice the causal model, which is the situation model. Checking an answer for factual completeness alone misses this.

## Candidate rules for the harness

1. **Never compress referents.** When a pronoun or "this" could point to more than one antecedent, repeat the noun. Use one term per concept throughout an answer and do not vary it for style. Conceptual overlap between consecutive sentences is cheap precision.
2. **Scale explanatory links to the reader's evident knowledge.**
   - If the user's wording shows domain expertise (jargon used correctly, a narrow question), state conclusions and skip the bridging background and causal steps they can infer.
   - If the user shows little background, make the causal and logical relations explicit, with connectives such as *because*, *so* and *therefore* and with one bridging sentence per unstated step.
   - When unsure, give the expert-length core first and one optional bridging line after it.
3. **For a novice, check situation-level understanding, not just facts.** Before finalizing an answer to a newcomer, ask: "Could the reader use this to answer a *why* or *what-if* question?" If the answer lists facts without the relations between them, add the missing relation, not more facts.
