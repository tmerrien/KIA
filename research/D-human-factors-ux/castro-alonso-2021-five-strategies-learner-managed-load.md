## Core claim
The review covers five ways to reduce needless load in learning materials, each using a CLT effect or a CTML principle:
- multimedia: add visuals;
- split attention / spatial contiguity: integrate text with the visuals it refers to;
- redundancy / coherence: remove non-essential material;
- signaling: cue what is essential;
- transient information / segmenting: segment or pace animations.

Each strategy can be applied by the instructor, who redesigns the material, or by the learner, who self-manages it (moving text onto pictures, summarising, highlighting, pausing). The evidence on which works better is mixed. The authors predict, from the expertise-reversal effect, that novices gain more when the instructor applies the strategy, and more expert learners gain more when they apply it themselves. They say this section is "somewhat speculative".

## Key evidence (as reported in the review; primary studies not re-read unless noted)
- **Split attention, pooled:** Ginns (2006) found d = 0.72 over 37 effect sizes; Schroeder & Cenkci (2018) found g = 0.63 over 58 independent effect sizes. The review calls both "large" on Kraft's (2020) benchmarks. One web summary gives Ginns as d = 0.85; that figure is UNVERIFIED and may be a different subset.
- **Split attention does not always replicate.** Re-runs with "exactly the same or slightly adjusted instructional materials" found no difference in learning or load (Cammeraat et al. 2020; Pouw et al. 2019). The review suggests learner expertise as a possible cause (Kalyuga et al. 1998).
- **Definition of redundancy:** "redundancy occurs when one source of information—either the text or the visualizations—could be understood independently; instructional materials are not redundant when understanding depends on integrating both sources" (section "Instructor-Managed Solution: Remove Redundant or Nonessential Information", citing Kalyuga & Sweller 2014).
- **Less is more, secondary-cited:** in Mayer et al. (1996), students who studied only a short visual summary (simple illustrations with brief captions) learned "at least as well or better" than students who read the full chapter, with or without the summary. "Adding text to the visual summary made it less effective." The primary study was not read (UNVERIFIED in detail).
- **Redundant on-screen text:** Mayer, Heiser & Lonn (2001) found that on-screen text that "summarized or was identical to" the narration lowered both retention and transfer. Secondary-cited; the primary was not read.
- **"Sometimes little redundancy is helpful"** (de Koning et al. 2017; Mayer & Johnson 2008; Yue et al. 2013). In de Koning et al. (2017), short on-screen labels improved retention but not performance. Short, non-identical redundancy is the documented exception.
- **Signals can themselves be redundant.** Signals that *add* elements, such as an on-screen hand, sometimes lowered scores (Castro-Alonso et al. 2015, 2018; Schroeder & Traxler 2017). The review reads this as "a negative redundancy effect". Signals that add nothing (contrast, spotlight, colour, zoom) may work better because they "do[] not add to the number of elements to be processed".
- **Segment only at idea boundaries.** Segmenting helps only when each segment "has internal logic so it can be processed by working memory as a single element". Mid-sentence breaks are "ineffective". In Boltz (1992), commercial breaks placed inside idea units hurt plot memory and breaks placed between units did not. Pooled segmenting effects: transfer d = 0.35, retention d = 0.42 (Rey et al. 2019). Learner pace control: transfer d = 0.45, retention d = 0.19 (n.s.).
- **Learners managing redundancy themselves:** Mirza et al. (2020) ran three experiments in which children were taught to remove redundant text boxes. There was no significant difference from instructor-removed or redundant materials. Summarising helps only when learners can find the main ideas. Spirgel & Delaney (2016) found writing summaries was no better than restudying.
- **Discrepancy with the primary source (verified against the full-text note on Chandler & Sweller 1991):** the review says students in the 1991 heart study "learned best when the text and diagrams were spatially separated ... (rather than integrated) or when students only learned from the diagrams". Our reading of the primary paper (Exp. 5) finds the opposite ranking for the separated format. It was the slowest (158.8 s), and its mean fell "below a quarter of the diagram-only group". Diagram-only was best. Whether integrated beat separated on scores is not recorded in our note (UNVERIFIED). Either way, the review's "separated learned best" claim is not supported by the primary paper and should be treated as a secondary-source distortion. The point they agree on, that removing the redundant text was best, holds.

## Relevance to AI answer style
- **The assistant is the "instructor".** The review predicts, without confirming, that novices cannot reliably self-manage load. They do not know which parts are redundant, and they write weak summaries without guidance. An assistant that hands over everything and expects the user to skim (learner-managed redundancy) moves the selection work to the reader who is least able to do it. For an expert asker, learner-managed formats such as a terse answer plus pointers are more defensible. This is the expertise-reversal split again, now applied to *who does the cutting*.
- **Precision vs. compression, restated:** redundancy is defined by *whether a source makes sense alone*, not by word count. A short visual summary beat the full chapter (precision: an essential, self-sufficient statement). Cutting text that is needed to make sense of something else is not removing redundancy; it is compression that breaks understanding.
- **Formatting devices can add load.** An added "pointing hand" signal behaved like redundant content. Applied to text (an extrapolation, UNTESTED for prose answers): bold on the key term that is already there is a "signal without added elements". Callout boxes, "Note:", "Important:" and emoji markers add elements to process and may cost more than they give.
- **Chunk at idea boundaries.** The segmenting finding (breaks help only between idea units) carries over to splitting an answer into bullets or sections. Each bullet should be one complete idea. Splitting one causal chain across bullets is the "mid-sentence break" failure.
- **A secondary summary got the primary result wrong.** A well-cited 2021 review misstates the 1991 result, which supports the harness's rule against citing secondary paraphrase as if it were the finding.

## Candidate rules for the harness
1. **Do the curation yourself; don't hand it to the reader.** Decide what is essential and cut the rest before answering. Do not include extra material "in case" and leave the user to filter it, especially when the asker seems new to the topic. Leave more for the reader to work out only when the user is clearly an expert.
2. **One bullet, one complete idea.** Put section and bullet breaks between idea units, never inside one. A cause and its effect, or a step and its condition, belong in the same bullet.
3. **Signal by marking, not by adding.** Emphasise the key term that is already in the sentence (for example, bold it once). Don't add extra signals such as "Note:", "Important:", callout boxes or repeated recap lines. Each one is another element the reader must process.
