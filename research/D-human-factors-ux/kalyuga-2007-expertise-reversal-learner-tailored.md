## Core claim (from the abstract)
Since the mid-1990s, research inside a cognitive load framework has studied how learners' prior knowledge interacts with the effectiveness of instructional techniques; this line of work is the "expertise reversal effect". The pattern of findings matches the Aptitude-Treatment Interaction (ATI) tradition that began in the mid-1960s, so the effect is a cognitive-load account of a much older observation. The review covers four topics: the empirical findings, their cognitive-load interpretation, the link to ATI, and what they imply for designing learner-tailored systems, including "some recent experimental attempts of implementing these findings into realistic adaptive learning environments" (abstract).

## Relevance to AI answer style
- **Brevity depends on the reader.** This review is the canonical statement that redundancy, and therefore brevity, is a property of the reader-text pair. The same explanatory sentence is essential for a novice and redundant for an expert. For an assistant, PRECISION means choosing the term and level of detail that fit the reader's current knowledge. COMPRESSION that drops a definition a novice needs is a failure. So is padding that forces an expert to process a definition they already hold (the expert's schema and the text then duplicate each other).
- **ATI gives the "it depends" a long history.** No answer format is best for everyone. Format has to be conditioned on evidence about the user. In the 1960s-80s ATI literature, those interactions often failed to replicate (Cronbach's well-known caution). This is UNVERIFIED as a claim of this paper, and it argues for adapting on *observed* signals rather than on stereotypes.
- **Adaptation should follow evidence.** The "adaptive learning environments" strand (rapid diagnosis, then tailored and faded guidance) is a direct model for a harness. Infer expertise from the user's own wording and earlier turns. Start terse for users who show expertise, and expand when a follow-up shows a gap.

## Candidate rules for the harness
1. **Calibrate detail to evidence, not to a default.** Before adding definitions, background or worked steps, look for evidence of the user's expertise: terminology they used correctly, code they wrote, earlier turns. Leave out explanations the user has already shown they hold. Keep them in when there is no such evidence.
2. **Fade guidance across turns.** Give the step-by-step version first to a user who shows little background. As the user shows competence, move to bare results, the "worked example to problem solving" fading in the Renkl strand. Never switch abruptly to fully terse mid-explanation.
3. **Don't confuse a precise term with a missing gloss.** Use the exact technical term (precision). If the user may not know it, add a one-clause gloss once, at first use, and then use the bare term. Never repeat the gloss for an expert, and never replace the term with a vague paraphrase.
