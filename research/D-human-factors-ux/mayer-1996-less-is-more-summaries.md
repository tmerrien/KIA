## Core claim (max 3 sentences)

A summary made of a short sequence of captioned simple illustrations of the main causal steps did as well as or better than the full 600-word textbook lesson on recall of those steps and on problem-solving transfer. It also did as well as or better than the full text plus the summary. It worked only as a coordinated word-plus-picture unit. Removing either the captions or the illustrations eliminated the benefit, and adding text back to the summary reduced it.

## Key evidence

- **Abstract (verbatim, via OpenAlex).**
  - "In a series of 3 experiments, college students who read a summary that contained a sequence of short captions with simple illustrations depicting the main steps in the process of lightning recalled these steps and solved transfer problems as well as or better than students who received the full text along with the summary or the full text alone."
  - "In Experiment 2, taking away the illustrations or the captions eliminated the effectiveness of the summary."
  - "In Experiment 3, adding text to the summary reduced its effectiveness."
  - The design implications concern "the need for conciseness, coherence, and coordination in presenting scientific explanations."
  - ERIC gives the sample as 163 college students in total.
- **What "summary" meant.** This is the point the queue entry asked about.
  - The full lesson was about 600 words with five illustrations explaining the cause-and-effect chain of lightning (opening paragraph).
  - The summary consisted of five annotated illustrations whose captions totalled about 80 words, "taken from the standard passage" (Clark & Mayer 2011, pp. 170-171). Mayer (2002), Fig. 4, reproduces the captions. Example: "1. Warm moist air rises, water vapor condenses and forms a cloud." ... "5. Positively charged particles from the ground rush upward along the same path."
  - So the summary was *extractive selection of the essential causal steps*, kept verbatim, plus a picture for each step. It was not a paraphrase, and it was not an abbreviated or telegraphic version of the full text. Every step of the causal chain survived. What was cut was elaboration around the steps.
- **Effect sizes (Mayer 2002, Table XVI, "Eliminate irrelevant words").** These are transfer-test effect sizes for summary vs. full text:

  | Experiment | Effect size |
  |---|---|
  | Exp. 1 | 1.47 |
  | Exp. 2 | 0.93 |
  | Exp. 3 | 2.31 |
  | Median | 1.47 |

  - The same chapter's Table XI lists Mayer et al. (1996), Exp. 2, as a *multimedia* effect of d = 1.39 for words plus pictures vs. words alone. That is the captions-only summary vs. the captioned-illustration summary.
  - Clark & Mayer (2011, p. 171) say summary readers sometimes produced "twice as many steps in the causal chain on the retention test and twice as many solutions on the transfer test."
- **Discrepancy to note.** Mayer & Fiorella (2014), already covered in this track, say the summary beat the full lesson on transfer in "2 of 3" tests. Mayer (2002) lists three positive effect sizes. The abstract's wording, "as well as or better than", fits the view that not every comparison was significant. Without the article body, which experiment was the null is UNVERIFIED.
- **Theory as the authors frame it (Mayer 2002, pp. 115-116).** Two theories make opposite predictions:
  - Information-delivery theory predicts that more material means more learning.
  - The cognitive theory of multimedia learning predicts that the added material hurts in three ways. It distracts attention (selecting), it disrupts the causal chain by putting material between the steps (organizing), and it diverts the learner toward the wrong prior knowledge (integrating).
  - The summary removes all three costs.
- **Boundary conditions.**
  - The participants were novice college students learning one short causal-mechanism lesson in a laboratory, with an immediate test.
  - Mayer & Fiorella (2014, p. 308) caution that minimizing detail suits novices, while experts may need more detail (expertise reversal; see the Kalyuga 2003 note).

## Relevance to AI answer style

- This is the cleanest experimental case for **precision, not compression**. The winning summary kept every causal step and the original wording of each step, and paired each step with a supporting representation. It cut only the connective elaboration.
- The two failure directions both appear in Experiments 2 and 3:
  - **Over-compression.** Dropping the captions or the pictures, so that part of the core representation is missing, killed the benefit.
  - **Over-expansion.** Adding the full text back, even alongside the summary, reduced the benefit.
- For an assistant, the "summary" condition corresponds to an ordered list of the essential steps or claims, each stated once in the exact terms, with a single supporting artifact (code, a diagram or an example) attached to the step it explains.
- The "full text plus summary" condition corresponds to a common LLM habit: a long prose explanation followed by a recap. The study suggests the recap does not rescue the prose. Removing the prose does better.
- Caveat on the evidence: novices, an immediate test, and a single causal mechanism. Do not apply it to expert users who ask for the reasoning behind an answer.

## Candidate rules for the harness

1. **Keep every step, cut the elaboration.** For an explanation of a process or mechanism, give the ordered causal steps, one short sentence each, in the precise terms. Elaboration that adds no step is removed, not merely moved into an appendix. (A 600-to-80-word summary beat the full lesson, with transfer d = 0.93-2.31.)
2. **Do not pair a long explanation with a summary of it.** Choose the summary. Add detail only when the user asks, or when the user is clearly an expert. (In Exp. 3, adding text to the summary reduced its effectiveness, and full text plus summary did no better than summary alone.)
3. **Compression check: if removing a part breaks a step, keep it.** The summary's captions and illustrations were each necessary. A shorter answer that drops a step, the term that names it, or the example that grounds it is compression, not concision. (Exp. 2: removing either component eliminated the effect.)
