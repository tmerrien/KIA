## Core claim (max 3 sentences)

Learning fails when essential processing plus extraneous processing (caused by irrelevant material or by a confusing layout) exceeds working-memory capacity, which the chapter calls "extraneous overload". Five design methods reduce this load. Two remove material: coherence cuts extraneous material, and redundancy removes duplicated printed text. Three guide attention: signaling, spatial contiguity and temporal contiguity. The guiding premise is "less is more": "Extraneous material should be ruthlessly weeded out whenever possible so that the core of essential material is salient to the learner" (p. 307).

## Key evidence

The chapter counts studies whose outcome measure is problem-solving transfer, and computes Cohen's d.

| Principle | Definition (verbatim gist) | Tests supporting it | Median d |
|---|---|---|---|
| Coherence | learn more deeply when extraneous material is excluded rather than included | 23 of 23 | 0.86 |
| Signaling | learn more deeply when cues are added that highlight the organization of the essential material | 24 of 28 | 0.41 (verbal 0.50; visual 0.36) |
| Redundancy | graphics + narration beats graphics + narration + identical on-screen text | 16 of 16 | 0.86 |
| Spatial contiguity | corresponding words and pictures near rather than far | 22 of 22 | 1.10 |
| Temporal contiguity | corresponding narration and animation simultaneous rather than successive | 9 of 9 | 1.22 |

(One search snippet gave signaling as "22 of 22, d = 1.10". That is wrong; the chapter gives those figures for spatial contiguity.)

- **What "extraneous" means here.** Extraneous material means "words and pictures that are not relevant to achieving the instructional objective, such as interesting stories or pictures" (p. 280). The coherence manipulation removed seductive details that were accurate and interesting but off-goal. Example: a football player struck by lightning, inserted into a lesson on how lightning forms (Table 12.2; Harp & Mayer 1997, 1998, all d > 1.0). The manipulation also removed quantitative details from qualitative explanations (Mayer & Jackson 2005, d about 0.7-0.97).
- **Summaries beat full text.** Mayer et al. (1996) found that a concise summary of the main steps outperformed the full textbook lesson on transfer in 2 of 3 tests.
- **Rey (2012) meta-analysis**, as cited in the chapter: d = 0.65 for removing extraneous text and d = 0.83 for removing extraneous illustrations. The effect was stronger for learners with low working-memory capacity, for system-paced lessons, and for highly distracting details.
- **Signaling adds no content.** In the airplane-lift script (Table 12.3) the signals are an opening sentence that lists the three steps, a heading before each step, and spoken emphasis on key words. The authors say: "the signals do not add or remove any new information but rather highlight the essential material" (p. 285). Two further examples: an organizing sentence that lists 7 steps, with "(1)" numbering, gave d = 0.34 (Harp & Mayer 1998); a narrated outline plus headings gave d = 0.60-0.70 (Mautone & Mayer 2001).
- **Signaling boundary: moderation.** Stull & Mayer (2007) found that 27 graphic organizers gave d = -0.03, while 18 gave d = 0.58 and 10 gave d = 0.45. The chapter reads this as evidence that "signaling should be used in moderation" (p. 293).
- **Other signaling boundaries.** Signaling helped low-skill readers (d = 0.42-0.65) but not high-skill readers (d = -0.12, -0.31) (Naumann et al. 2007). Highlighting helped with complex displays but not simple ones (Jeung et al. 1997).
- **Redundancy boundaries.** The effect disappears or reverses when learners are experienced, when the on-screen text is short, or when there are no graphics. A key word placed next to the relevant part of a graphic improved retention (d = 0.47-0.70) without hurting transfer (Mayer & Johnson 2008). The chapter also places its own coherence principle inside Sweller and Kalyuga's broader redundancy principle: "the same information is presented in multiple forms or is unnecessarily elaborated" (p. 299).
- **Spatial contiguity heuristic:** "When a multimedia message says, 'See Figure 1,' there is a good chance that the spatial contiguity principle has been violated" (p. 307). The effect is strongest when each source is unintelligible on its own and the material is complex.
- **Temporal contiguity, chunk size.** The effect can be eliminated or reversed when material comes in small chunks that do not exceed working memory (Mayer et al. 1999; Moreno & Mayer 1999). It was negligible when the learner controlled the pace (Michas & Berry 2000).
- **Limits the authors admit.** Most studies use short lab lessons on science mechanisms and measure only transfer. Cognitive load is rarely measured directly. The authors also caution: "minimizing detail ... may be a good approach for novices, but more experienced learners may require a greater amount of detail" (p. 308), which ties the effect to expertise reversal.

## Relevance to AI answer style

- **Coherence names the real target of concision.** Removing a "seductive detail", meaning a true and interesting aside that does not serve the user's goal, is the single strongest lever in the chapter (23 of 23 tests, d = 0.86). The removed material is accurate. Cutting it is not lossy COMPRESSION, because no essential proposition is dropped; it is a cut of extraneous material. The chapter's operational test for what is extraneous is "relevant to achieving the instructional objective". For an assistant, that means the user's goal, not the topic.
- **Signaling separates structure from content.** Signals "do not add or remove any new information" (p. 285): an opening line that lists the parts, one short heading per part, and emphasis on the key term. That is a way to make an answer more scannable without adding words of substance. It is also bounded: too many signals erased the effect (27 organizers gave d of about 0). Bolding everything signals nothing.
- **The bold key word maps onto PRECISION.** The verbal-signaling effect works by emphasizing the exact key terms that carry the explanation (bold words in Table 12.3). That is what precision does: the one exact term that carries the idea is the term worth highlighting.
- **Boundary conditions argue for calibrating to the reader.** Gains are largest for novices, low-skill readers and complex material. They shrink or reverse for experts, simple material and short content. An answer to a short or simple question needs no headings. An expert may want more detail, not less (p. 308).
- **Spatial contiguity applies to text plus code.** "See Figure 1" is a warning sign. Put the explanation next to the code line or table cell it refers to, and do not write a separate paragraph that points back to it.

## Candidate rules for the harness

1. **Cut seductive details.** Before sending, delete every sentence that is true and interesting but that the user's stated goal does not need: background, trivia, history, related-but-unasked facts. The test is whether the user would be worse at their task without it, not whether it is accurate.
2. **Signal structure sparingly.** For multi-part answers, start with one sentence naming the parts, then use one short label per part. Bold only the one or two load-bearing terms per section. Omit headings and bold entirely for short or single-point answers, because over-signaling cancels the benefit.
3. **Keep explanation next to its referent.** Put each comment beside the code line, table row or step it explains. Do not write "see above" or "as shown in the snippet" with the explanation detached from the thing.
