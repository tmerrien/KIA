# Hinds (1999), The curse of expertise: experts underestimate novice difficulty, and debiasing does not help

## Core claim
Experts predicting how long novices take on a complex task are *less* accurate than intermediates or the novices themselves, because their expertise makes them underestimate how hard the task is for novices. The underestimation resists the debiasing techniques Hinds tested. People with intermediate expertise may be the best predictors of novice performance.

## Key evidence
- **From the abstract (verified):** "In Study 1, experts, intermediate users, and novices predicted the time it would take novices to complete a complex task. In Study 2, expertise was experimentally manipulated. In both studies, those with more expertise were worse predictors of novice performance times and were resistant to debiasing techniques intended to reduce underestimation." The abstract adds that experts "may have a cognitive handicap that leads to underestimating the difficulty novices face."
- **Framing in the introduction (verified):** the stakes are applied ones. "Marketers and designers predict consumers' abilities to master the use of new products. Teachers predict students' efforts..." Predictive errors cause "consumer dissatisfaction, and student frustration or boredom."
- **Task (secondary, UNVERIFIED):** S. Beilock, *Psychology Today* blog "The curse of expertise" (Mar 2011), https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/blog/choke/201103/the-curse-expertise. In this account, salespeople of a new cell-phone technology, customers with some experience, and people with no experience estimated how long a new user would need to master the phone. The novices then learned it "using only the written instructions." Beilock reports that novices took "around 30 minutes," that salespeople estimated "less than 13 minutes," and that the customers with some experience were "the most accurate predictors." A search-engine summary gave a different figure (a 12.27-min median, Study 1), so the numbers are inconsistent and are NOT used here.
- **Debiasing methods (UNVERIFIED):** secondary accounts say the methods included having experts recall their own early learning. The paper's actual manipulations were not seen.

- **Tullis, J. G., & Feder, B. (2023).** The "curse of knowledge" when predicting others' knowledge. *Memory & Cognition* 51, 1214-1234. doi:10.3758/s13421-022-01382-3. **Full text read** at https://education.arizona.edu/sites/default/files/2025-11/tullis-feder-2022-Jonathan-Tullis-PASS.pdf. It cites Hinds (1999) as evidence that the bias spans business and other fields. It states that "eliminating or reducing the expertise bias is difficult," citing warnings (Pohl & Hell 1996), perspective-focus instructions (Damen et al. 2020) and incentives (Camerer et al. 1989; Kennedy 1995). Its own result refines Hinds: "Experimentally reducing reliance on one's own knowledge did not improve the accuracy of estimates." The failure arises "not because estimators rely too heavily on their own experiences, but because estimators lack diagnostic cues about others' knowledge." Lowering the salience of one's own knowledge (not answering first) did improve calibration, but resolution did not improve.

## Relevance to AI answer style
- An assistant is the extreme "expert" in Hinds' design. It holds the whole domain and has no memory of being a novice, so the curse should be expected to apply in full. The default error points one way: it *underestimates* how many steps, definitions and intermediate results the reader needs.
- The debiasing result is the key point for the harness. "Imagine you are a novice" and "recall your own early learning" are the closest human analogues of a self-prompt, and they did not work. Tullis & Feder explain why: removing your own perspective leaves nothing in its place. Accuracy comes from **diagnostic cues about the actual reader**, not from introspection.
- This separates precision from compression. Leaving out a needed step or definition is *compression driven by the curse of knowledge*: it feels concise to the writer and costs the reader. Precision means keeping the exact term and calibrating the scaffolding to evidence about the reader.
- The intermediate-expertise finding, together with Hinds et al. (2001), suggests a middle register: concrete steps plus the one key abstract term. That serves both the immediate task (concrete) and transfer (abstract).

## Candidate rules for the harness
1. **Calibrate to evidence, not introspection.** Estimate the user's level from observable cues in the conversation: the terms they use correctly, the code they show, the questions they ask, explicit statements. Do not use a self-simulated "novice view." When the cues are absent and the gap matters, ask one short question or state the assumed level ("Assuming you know X; if not, see below").
2. **Default to correcting toward more steps.** When the reader's level is uncertain, assume the bias runs toward under-explaining. Include the one intermediate step or definition an expert would skip, and make it skippable (a brief gloss or a final "if X is new" line). Do not pad the whole answer.
3. **Concrete first, then the abstract term.** For how-to answers, give concrete, actionable steps. Name the general principle once, by its exact term, for transfer (Hinds et al. 2001). Do not answer with the principle alone.
