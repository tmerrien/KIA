## Correction to how this work is cited in the corpus
The queue entry, `chen-kalyuga-sweller-2017-element-interactivity-expertise-reversal.md` and `_rules.md` describe the comparison as **integrated glosses versus footnotes**, with footnotes or separable glosses helping experts. **The primary source does not support that.** Every experiment compared:

1. **Explanatory notes:** a Modern English paraphrase or interpretation in parentheses directly beneath each line of verse.
2. **Control:** "a conventional unguided original text condition," meaning the original verse **with no notes of any kind** (journal abstract; thesis p. 132).

No footnote, endnote, glossary or separate-gloss condition was tested. The thesis argues the opposite of "footnotes help experts": "Despite readers' level of expertise, conventional footnotes and endnotes will always be a source of split attention" (p. 207). What the study shows is narrower: **for experts, no gloss beat an inline gloss they could not skip.** The integrated-versus-separate-glossary test with a reversal by expertise is Yeung, Jin & Sweller (1998), which this study builds on (proposed below).

## Core claim
Line-by-line paraphrase built into a hard text lowers cognitive load and improves comprehension for novices. For experts, the same notes are redundant and reverse the effect, causing higher load and worse comprehension. The experts' think-aloud reports trace the cost to cross-checking each gloss against their own reading and to the broken flow of reading. The authors conclude that explanations should fade as readers gain knowledge, ideally through learner-controlled, expandable glosses.

## Key evidence
Material: an extract of character speeches (Othello 5.2.1-23; Romeo and Juliet in Exp. 4) with a short plot summary at the top for both groups. Reading time was 7 minutes, followed by a 9-point mental-effort rating and a comprehension test of simple and complex short-answer items, marked by two independent markers. Groups were matched on pre-test and self-rated knowledge.

| Thesis exp. (paper exp.) | Readers | Notes vs original text |
|---|---|---|
| 1 (not in paper) | 24 drama-institute students with some prior knowledge; notes in parentheses but visually distinct | No significant differences. Load F(1,22)=2.65, p=.118; complex items p=.082 (pp. 137-149) |
| 2 (1) | 20 Year-10 students, complete novices on Othello; notes redesigned to be "tightly integrated" so the two texts were "not visually distinguishable" | Notes better on every measure. Load 3.70 vs 5.40, F(1,18)=9.82, d=1.40; simple items 5.5 vs 3.2/10, d=1.27; complex items 5.4 vs 3.4/12, d=1.95 (pp. 150-158) |
| 3 (2) | 22 Shakespeare experts (actors, directors, professors; mean age 46; all had used Othello recently); same materials | **Reversal.** Load 6.55 vs 1.73, F(1,20)=50.16, d=3.02; simple items 6.00 vs 8.36, d=1.65; complex items 4.91 vs 9.55/12, d=2.94 (pp. 159-170) |
| 4 (3) | 20 Year-10 novices, Romeo and Juliet | Notes better. Load d=1.83; simple items d=1.20; complex items d=1.08 (pp. 171-185) |
| 5 (not in paper) | 20 English-literature undergraduates (intermediate) | Lower load with notes (2.90 vs 4.80, d=1.50) but no difference in comprehension: simple items d=0.49, complex items d=0.30, both n.s. (pp. 186-194) |

- **Expert verbal protocols** (retrospective, 11 per group; Table 3-a, pp. 164-170):
  - Seven of 11 in the notes group **cross-checked** the notes against their own reading. One said: "'is that the interpretation that I would offer? Is that my reading of that line?' ... that takes me away from the text."
  - Seven reported **lost flow**: "the glosses were interrupting the flow of my reading."
  - Five found the notes hard to read. Two noted conflicting opinions ("you gloss it with dignity, but I read it as a stupid cliche"), and two found the notes **restrictive**: "they give you one thing ... What's brilliant about Shakespeare is he's open to so many interpretations."
  - One expert found the notes insightful.
  - All 11 control experts instead analysed imagery, rhythm and intention, visualised staging, or drew on performances they had seen.
- **Integration had to be tight to help novices.** Notes that were visibly separate in Exp. 1 let readers "decide whether they would actually process the explanatory notes" and gave no significant benefit. Making them indistinguishable produced the novice effect (pp. 133, 150). The same unskippability is what made them costly for experts.
- **Caveats.**
  - The samples are small (n=10-11 per cell), which the author names as a limitation (p. 208).
  - The expert effect sizes (d≈3) are unusually large.
  - Reading time was fixed while the notes roughly doubled the text, so the notes groups read the verse fewer times.
  - The comprehension items were scored against an expert answer key, so a gloss that fixes one interpretation may be penalised on open interpretive questions.
  - Only the cognitive-load-theory interpretation is tested.

## Relevance to AI answer style
- **A paraphrase of a precise text is not a gloss for an expert; it is a competing reading.** Experts did not just skip the extra words. They checked each paraphrase against their own reading, objected to it, and lost the thread (Table 3-a). When an assistant restates in plain words something the user has already said precisely (a stack trace, a spec clause, a term of art), an expert user reads the restatement as a claim to verify. That cost is larger than the word count. This is the PRECISION/COMPRESSION distinction from the reader's side: the exact term carries the idea, and a gloss that rephrases it makes the reader do a comparison.
- **Glosses narrow meaning.** "They give you one thing." When the source is deliberately open (a tradeoff, an ambiguous requirement), one inline paraphrase quietly commits to one reading. Name the ambiguity instead of paraphrasing it away.
- **What it does *not* show.** It does not show that footnoted or end-placed glosses are fine for experts; that was never tested. The best expert condition here was **no gloss at all**. The authors' preferred design is learner-controlled expansion: glosses hidden until asked for (pp. 199, 203, 207). For chat this suggests omitting glosses for experts and offering them for novices. A trailing glossary block is an untested middle ground, not a finding.
- **Intermediate readers are the hard case.** Notes lowered their load but did not improve comprehension (Exp. 5). When the reader's level is unclear, inline explanation buys comfort, not understanding, so put the precise core first and keep any gloss short.
- **Transfer limits.** These are 7-minute reading-comprehension tests on literary verse, not question-and-answer tasks. Carrying the findings to an assistant's answers is extrapolation.

## Candidate rules for the harness
1. **No inline paraphrase of what an expert user already said precisely.** If the user's message shows command of the domain (correct technical terms, a quoted spec or trace), answer in their terms. Do not interleave plain-language restatements of their input or of standard terms. Such restatements invite line-by-line cross-checking.
2. **Glosses go behind a choice, not in the reading path.** When a novice might need a term explained, give the precise term with at most a one-clause gloss on first use, or offer more detail at the end. Never interleave a paraphrase with each line of quoted code, legal text or specification unless the user asked for a line-by-line explanation.
3. **Do not collapse a deliberately open source into one paraphrase.** When the text being explained admits several readings, state that it does and list the readings briefly, rather than glossing it with one interpretation.
