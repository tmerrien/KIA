## Core claim
CLT's claim that working memory is limited is not in dispute. Its three-way split into intrinsic, extraneous and germane load is a different matter: it is not cleanly separable, either in concept or in measurement. Which load a feature counts as depends on the learner's expertise and on the learning goal, so the theory "can account for nearly every situation" post hoc and is "hard or even impossible to falsify" (p. 125). Its lasting design value is narrower and older than the theory: match material to prior knowledge, "avoid non-essential and confusing information", and stimulate deep processing (p. 126).

## Key evidence
- **Intrinsic load is not fixed by element count** (pp. 106-107).
  - Some content is harder even with the same number of elements and the same interactivity. Examples are Tower of Hanoi variants (Klahr & Robinson 1981), ontological category shifts (Chi 1992, 2005), and conflicting or ambiguous relations (Campbell 1988).
  - Sequencing techniques do reduce intrinsic load: simple-to-complex, part-whole and whole-part (van Merrienboer et al. 2003; Pollock et al. 2002; Gerjets et al. 2004). This contradicts the 1998 premise that it "cannot be directly influenced".
- **What counts as "extraneous" is unstable** (pp. 108-109). de Jong gives four problems:
  - (a) Redundant dual representations, such as a graph plus its formula, can drive germane translation between the two (Ainsworth 2006).
  - (b) Extraneous load cannot reach zero. Integrating everything to avoid split attention can produce "unreadable" displays, and "having information in different places is then preferable to cluttering one place with all the information" (p. 109).
  - (c) Extraneous load depends on the learner's familiarity with the material's conventions.
  - (d) Redundant information "is advantageous as long as it is short and placed near the information to which it refers" (Mayer & Johnson 2008, cited p. 109).
  - Conclusion (p. 109): "it is not always evident which characteristics of material can be regarded as being extraneous."
- **Germane load is a post-hoc label** (p. 109). In 1998, the variability effect was explained as higher load that "must have been germane". A replication found the transfer gain without any rise in rated load (de Croock et al. 1998).
- **The categories shift with expertise and goal** (pp. 110-111). "A cognitive load that is germane for a novice may be extraneous for an expert" (Paas et al. 2004, quoted p. 111). Structure-emphasising study counted as extraneous when the test only needed isomorphic problems (Gerjets & Scheiter 2003).
- **Rewording is not an intrinsic change** (pp. 111-112). DeLeeuw & Mayer (2008) "manipulated intrinsic load" by making sentences more complex while the content stayed the same. de Jong argues this moves extraneous load, not intrinsic load.
- **Measurement problems** (pp. 114-119).
  - The usual instrument is Paas's (1992) one-item, 9-point effort scale. Studies vary its scale points, its anchors (effort vs. difficulty) and when it is given.
  - It gives only relative values and cannot detect overload: "all conditions in a study could show overload" (p. 118).
  - The instructional-efficiency formula E = (P - R)/sqrt(2) is often printed wrongly. As originally defined, it measures the quality of the resulting knowledge, not the efficiency of the instruction (pp. 119-121).
- **External validity** (pp. 121-122). Lab studies are short (5-6 minute lessons) and ban offloading, such as using a notepad. In real use, learners avoid instantaneous overload by working through parts sequentially and offloading to external aids (Bodemer & Faust 2006).

## Relevance to AI answer style
This paper limits how literally the harness can use CLT's vocabulary for "padding vs. needed content":
- **"Extraneous" depends on the reader, not on the text alone.** The same sentence can be scaffolding for a novice and padding for an expert, which is the expertise-reversal effect in de Jong's framing. A rule like "cut extraneous material" works only if the assistant first estimates who is reading. It is not a property the assistant can read off the draft.
- **Redundancy is not always waste.** A short restatement placed *next to* its referent can help (Mayer & Johnson 2008). Long or distant restatement is what hurts. So the line between precision and padding is about length and placement, not about whether a sentence restates something at all.
- **Rewording is not cutting content.** Making sentences simpler, as DeLeeuw & Mayer did, changes presentation cost and leaves the content alone. This is the clearest operational split between PRECISION and COMPRESSION. Plain, exact phrasing lowers extraneous cost with zero loss of content. Removing a step or a condition changes the task.
- **Over-integration is its own cost.** Packing every qualification inline to avoid "see above" can make a passage unreadable (p. 109). Sometimes a separate, labelled block is better than one dense sentence.
- **Readers offload.** Chat output stays on screen and can be re-read, so instantaneous working-memory overload matters less than in timed lab tasks (pp. 121-122). Structure that supports re-reading and lookup counts for more than raw brevity. This is an extrapolation; the paper does not test chat interfaces.
- **Don't over-claim.** Load-type language is loose. The harness should cite the specific effects (redundancy, split attention, expertise reversal), not "germane load", as its rationale.

## Candidate rules for the harness
1. **Judge padding against the reader, not the text.** Before cutting or adding an explanation, infer the user's expertise from their wording. A gloss that serves a novice is padding for an expert, and the reverse also holds: a bare term that serves an expert is a gap for a novice.
2. **Restate only short and adjacent.** A restatement is allowed only if it is brief and sits next to what it restates, such as a one-line takeaway right under a table. Never repeat content at length or far from its source.
3. **Simplify wording before deleting content.** To shorten an answer, first rephrase: shorter sentences, exact terms, no hedges. Delete only material the task does not need. Rephrasing lowers cost without loss; deleting a needed step is compression, not efficiency.
