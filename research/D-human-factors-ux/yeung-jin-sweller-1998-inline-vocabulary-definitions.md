## Core claim
The same in-text placement of vocabulary definitions can help or hurt, depending on the reader. For weak readers, definitions built into the passage remove the split attention of looking words up in a separate list, and comprehension improves. For strong readers the definitions are redundant: built in, they lower comprehension (abstract). The authors argue that a format is efficient only to the extent that it does not impose extraneous load, so no format is good for every reader.

## Key evidence (from the abstract, verbatim in substance)
Five experiments:

| Exp. | Readers | Comparison | Comprehension | Vocabulary learning |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Grade 5, first language (L1) | Explanatory notes vs none (as the abstract implies) | Notes improved it ("high-level processing") | No improvement ("low-level processing") |
| 2 | Grade 5, L1 | Definitions built into the passage vs a separate vocabulary list | **Integrated better** | **Integrated worse** |
| 3 | Adults | Same comparison | **Integrated worse** | **Integrated better** |
| 4 | Grade 8, low-ability ESL | Same comparison | Like Exp. 2 (integrated better) | Like Exp. 2 (integrated worse) |
| 5 | Grade 8, high-ability ESL | Same comparison | Like Exp. 3 (integrated worse) | Like Exp. 3 (integrated better) |

- **The result is a crossover on two outcomes, not just one reversal.** For weak readers, inline definitions help reading for meaning but hurt word learning. For strong readers it is the other way round. A summary such as "inline glosses hurt strong readers" leaves out that inline glosses still taught the strong readers more vocabulary.
- **The ESL experiments show the cause is skill, not age.** High-ability Grade 8 ESL readers behaved like the adults, and low-ability Grade 8 ESL readers behaved like the Grade 5 L1 readers. What reverses the effect is language proficiency relative to the text, not age or school level.
- **Layout and measures (secondary, Oksa 2009):**
  - **Layout:** the control placed a glossary at the bottom of the page, and the integrated format put definitions "above individual target words within the text" (Oksa 2009, p. 76). Elsewhere Oksa says the definitions were placed "next to target words" (pp. 67, 131). The exact layout is UNVERIFIED.
  - **Integration was partial.** Single-word definitions were placed by target words, not whole clauses rewritten, so readers still had to fit a separate word into the sentence (p. 131).
  - **Materials:** "very basic level primary texts", expository and narrative (pp. 67, 131).
  - **Load:** measured on a nine-point self-rating scale (p. 128).
  - **Framing:** the authors "did not formally conclude that an expertise reversal effect had taken place" (p. 131). The study was labelled an expertise reversal only later, by Kalyuga et al. (2003).
- **Two secondary-source errors.**
  - Oksa says the high-knowledge readers were "university students" (p. 76). The abstract says "adult readers", so the population is UNVERIFIED.
  - A Kalyuga et al. (2003) summary in this corpus says the notes "retarded learning" for knowledgeable readers. That is true of comprehension, but the abstract shows **better** vocabulary learning for those readers with integrated definitions.
- **Effect sizes, Ns, and whether reading time was fixed or self-paced: UNVERIFIED.** This matters because Oksa (2010) fixed reading time, which inflates any cost of added text.

## Relevance to AI answer style
- **The question is never "define or not". It is "define for whom, and for what".** The crossover shows that a gloss built into the text trades understanding the passage now against learning the terms. For a reader who lacks the term, an in-place definition makes the sentence intelligible. For a reader who has it, the definition is noise in the reading path. This is the PRECISION side of the distinction: the exact term carries the idea for the reader who knows it, and a gloss neither shortens nor improves it for that reader.
- **Use the user's demonstrated proficiency, not a guessed "level".** In Exps. 4-5, readers of the same age and grade split by ability. The signal an assistant has is the user's own wording: terms used correctly, quoted errors, the register of the question. Calibrate to that, not to a profile.
- **Teaching intent changes the answer.** When a user is trying to learn terminology ("what does X mean", "explain like I'm new"), inline definitions helped strong readers' vocabulary learning, and per Oksa, studying definitions alone was better still. So when the request is about the terms themselves, define them directly and do not bury the definitions in a long worked passage.
- **Moving a gloss to a separate list is not free for novices.** The separate list was the split-attention condition and hurt weak readers' comprehension. "Put the glossary at the end" helps experts and costs novices. For an uncertain reader, the safer form is a very short gloss at first use (an appositive of a few words), not a footnote-style list.
- **Transfer limits.** These were basic school texts with single-word definitions, measured by comprehension and vocabulary tests. Chat answers are not reading-comprehension passages, and none of this was tested on technical prose or on dialogue.

## Candidate rules for the harness
1. **Define a term in place only when the user's own wording suggests they lack it.** If the user has used the term, or a closely related one, correctly, use it bare. If there is no signal, add at most a short appositive gloss at first use, never a full sentence of definition, and never repeat the gloss.
2. **Do not move definitions to a trailing glossary for a reader who needs them.** A separate list forces look-ups and hurt weak readers. If a novice needs several terms, gloss each briefly where it first appears. Keep end-of-answer definition lists for when the user asked for definitions.
3. **When the user's goal is learning the terms, answer with the definitions directly.** Give a compact term-and-definition list or a contrastive definition, rather than explaining the terms in passing inside a long answer.
