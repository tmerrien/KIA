# Duggan & Payne (2011): Skim reading by satisficing (eye tracking)

## Citation
Duggan, G. B., & Payne, S. J. (2011). Skim reading by satisficing: evidence from eye tracking. In *CHI '11: Proceedings of the SIGCHI Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems*, pp. 1141-1150. New York: ACM. doi:10.1145/1978942.1979114

## Core claim
When skimmers have too little time to read everything, they still spend more time on the important sentences, and this improves their memory for those sentences. They manage this by **satisficing**: each paragraph (and each page) is read linearly until the rate of information gain drops below a threshold, then the reader skips to the next unit. Under these conditions the data fit satisficing better than Nielsen's F-pattern.

## Key evidence
- **Design.** N = 28 Manchester students, Tobii 1750 eye tracker. Two Scientific American texts (Hypnosis, 3134 words; ADHD, 3252 words), each split into 11 headed pages. The time limit (325 s and 313 s) required about 600 wpm to finish; normal reading is about 225 wpm. Participants were told to allocate their time to maximise a later memory-for-meaning test. Text importance was rated by 20 independent undergraduates (the 18 highest-rated sentences per text counted as important, the 18 lowest as unimportant).
- **Importance drives attention.** Total gaze time per character was 13.26 ms for important sentences and 8.95 ms for unimportant ones, t(27) = 4.52, p < .001, d = .55. The effect held for lines containing the sentences (10.12 vs 6.76 ms, d = .59) and for whole paragraphs (9.60 vs 6.76 ms, d = .57).
- **Attention drives retention.** Sentences that were later categorised correctly had longer gaze times than incorrect ones, F(1,27) = 17.25, p < .001, ηp² = .39. The effect did not interact with importance. Important + correct had the highest mean and SD (16.86, SD 11.35), which the authors read as occasional long dwelling on key material.
- **Within a paragraph the decline is gradual, not a step.** Gaze time fell linearly across lines, F(1,27) = 44.78, ηp² = .62. For 10 of the 11 lines, gaze time was more than half that of line 1. Each line was fixated at least once in 0.4 or more of cases, so many paragraphs were read to the end. The authors argue an F-pattern sweep would predict a step function instead.
- **Within a page the same gradual decline appears**, F(1,27) = 44.90, ηp² = .62. Readers appear to judge the page and the paragraph at the same time.
- **Weak left-edge scan.** In vertical stripes, only the leftmost tenth was slightly higher (F = 97.58 for the overall left-to-right decline, driven mostly by the short, ragged-right final tenth). The authors call the left-edge effect "not large."
- **Reconciling with the F-pattern.** The authors do *not* claim the F-pattern is wrong. They argue it may be a **sampling** behaviour: a brief evaluation that decides whether to stay on a page, seen on heterogeneous Web pages and homepages. Satisficing is what happens once a reader stays on relevant, homogeneous text.

## Relevance to AI answer style
- This is controlled, peer-reviewed evidence that a time-pressed reader reads **each paragraph from its start and leaves when the information rate drops**. The first sentence of every paragraph is therefore read most often, and it should carry that paragraph's point. This is precision placed where attention is highest. It is not compression: the authors explicitly say later content is still read when the opening sustains interest.
- It argues against reflexive front-loading of *everything*. "The writer's goal need not be to frontload the text with all the major points." Supporting detail can follow, provided each unit opens with information-dense content that keeps the reader's information-gain rate above threshold. Filler openings (restating the question, preamble, hedges) lower the early gain rate and invite a skip of the whole unit.
- Readers judge two levels at once (paragraph and page). For an assistant answer this maps to the section or paragraph level and the whole-response level. Headings and paragraph breaks set the units over which readers decide whether to stay. Overlong, multi-topic paragraphs put their later points at risk.
- It separates **sampling** (a brief evaluation: is this answer worth reading?) from **satisficing** (reading the relevant body). The first lines of a response serve the sampling decision. The body serves satisficing.
- Caveat: N = 28 students, two expository texts, with skimming forced by the time limit. The authors note it does not refute the F-pattern under other conditions.

## Candidate rules for the harness
1. **Lead every paragraph with its claim.** The first sentence of each paragraph or section must state that unit's main point in exact terms. Skimmers read paragraph openings most and leave when the information rate drops.
2. **Keep information rate high at the top of each unit, not just the top of the answer.** No throat-clearing, restating the question, or generic hedges at the start of a paragraph. Supporting detail may follow because readers who stay keep reading, so do not cut needed detail just to front-load.
3. **One topic per paragraph, with short headed sections for multi-part answers.** Readers decide to stay or skip at paragraph and section level, so a point buried late in a mixed paragraph is the most likely to be skipped.
