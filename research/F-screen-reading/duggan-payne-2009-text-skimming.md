- Full text read: the peer-reviewed author manuscript (green OA, University of Bath), https://purehost.bath.ac.uk/ws/files/354192/Duggan_Payne_JEPA_2009.pdf (linked from https://researchportal.bath.ac.uk/en/publications/text-skimming-the-process-and-effectiveness-of-foraging-through-t/). The manuscript notes that it "may not exactly replicate the final version". The figures, which are images, were not inspected. Tables 1-2 and all reported statistics were read.

## Core claim
When readers have only half the time a text needs, letting them skim the whole text improves memory for its **important** ideas, compared with reading an imposed half linearly. It does not improve memory for details or for inferences. The advantage disappears against a simple baseline: reading the first or second half of **every** paragraph. It also disappears when navigation between pages is costly. Skimmers read beginnings first, recursively, at the level of the document, the page and the paragraph. This fits a satisficing, patch-leaving account: read on until the information-gain rate falls below a threshold, then jump to the next unit.

## Key evidence
- **Materials and logic.** Two Scientific American texts (Hypnosis, 3134 words; ADHD, 3252 words), each split into 11 headed pages, with one macroproposition per page. The time limit (313 s and 325 s) required 600 wpm to finish; normal reading is about 225 wpm. The test was 54 true/false memory-for-meaning sentences per text (Important, Unimportant and Inference, rated by 20 independent undergraduates), scored as d'. Time is held constant and only the reader's discretion over allocation varies. This avoids the floor/scale problem in the earlier null results of Masson (1982), Carver (1984) and Dyson & Haselgrove (2000).
- **Exp 1 (N = 32; Skim vs first or second half of the document).** Skimming raised sensitivity overall, F(1,24) = 7.78, ηp² = .25. The gain came from Important sentences: d' 1.67 vs 1.13, F(1,24) = 10.99, ηp² = .31. There was no reliable gain for Unimportant (0.92 vs 0.86) or Inference sentences (1.38 vs 1.21). Skimmers also had a liberal "True" bias, F(1,24) = 17.09, ηp² = .42 (C = -.18 vs .06 for Important). The authors read this as a gap between readers' perceived and actual understanding. Structure-map (sentence location) accuracy showed no difference (about .22-.29 correct). 31 of 32 skimmers read pages in order once revisits were excluded.
- **Exp 2 (N = 32, Tobii 1750; Skim vs first or second half of every paragraph).** There was **no skimming advantage** (F < 1), and reading first halves of paragraphs was no better than reading second halves. The authors conclude that the Exp 1 benefit came mainly from *distributing* reading across the text, not from pinpointing important sentences: "spreading time allocation across the extent of a text, by sampling each paragraph, will be an effective strategy." Eye tracking (28 usable): skimmers spent more total fixation time per word on the first half of paragraphs, F(1,24) = 115.74, ηp² = .83, and on paragraphs higher on the page, F(2,48) = 14.97, ηp² = .34. First-pass times were "very short indeed", which shows a glance over the whole page before settling to read.
- **Earlier pages are read longer, in every condition and experiment** (Skim: ηp² = .59-.68). This holds even in linear half-text reading, so it is partly a property of the reader's gain curve, not only of skimming.
- **Exp 3A/3B (N = 32 each; website-like hierarchy).** In 3A, pages were reachable only up and down the tree. Skimming gave no benefit, and skimmers' Important d' was the lowest of all experiments. In 3B, links ran within topics and to the next topic. This restored the Exp 1 pattern: Important sentences improved after skimming, F(1,24) = 8.57, ηp² = .26, with no gain for the other types. Navigation cost alone removed the benefit of skimming.
- **Proposed mechanism (General Discussion).** Pages and documents show diminishing returns for two reasons. (1) Redundancy: the gist of a section can be inferred from whichever paragraph is read first. (2) Writers place important information early (Kieras, 1980; Strunk & White). Readers may judge **nested patches** at once (paragraph, section, document), for example by raising the threshold for every later paragraph after a weak one. This is offered as an untested extension.
- **Explicit limits.** The F-pattern was "not observed in Experiment 2". The authors suggest it is a *sampling* strategy suited to heterogeneous web pages. They also warn that deeper understanding needs "the micropropositions that set the context and provide coherence". Skimming did not help inferences.

## Relevance to AI answer style
- **Skimming captures the gist, not the reasoning.** Time-pressed readers kept the important points and lost the details and inferences. An answer that will be skimmed must state its conclusions **explicitly**. A conclusion the reader has to infer from the details is exactly what skimmers miss. This calls for precision (state the claim) rather than compression (leave the claim implicit in the evidence).
- **Distribution beats selection.** Reading half of every paragraph was as good as free skimming. The value lies in each unit carrying its own point, so that a reader sampling any part of every paragraph gets the macrostructure. Each paragraph should therefore be self-contained in its point. This matches the 2011 note's "lead every paragraph with its claim" rule, with a twist: the first half was *not* better than the second half here. Spread key content across units rather than piling it into the opening of the answer.
- **Diminishing returns are expected.** Readers spend the most time early in the answer, early in each section and early in each paragraph. Redundancy accelerates the decline: once the gist is inferable, later paragraphs that restate it get skipped and drag down the threshold for what follows. Recap and restatement are therefore costly, not neutral.
- **Navigation cost kills skimming.** When jumping between units was costly (3A), skimming gave no benefit. In a long answer, headings and clear unit boundaries are the "links" that let a reader jump to the next section cheaply. Unbroken walls of text work like the 3A hierarchy.
- **Overconfidence.** Skimmers showed a liberal "true" bias: they accepted false statements that sounded consistent. Caveats that matter should be stated where skimmers will see them, not left in late micro-detail.
- Caveats: student samples (N = 32 per experiment), two expository texts, forced 2x time pressure, and recognition memory rather than task performance. Exp 2's null result rests on a non-significant difference.

## Candidate rules for the harness
1. **State conclusions explicitly; never leave the key inference to the reader.** Skimmers retain stated main ideas but not inferences derivable from details, so every load-bearing conclusion needs its own sentence.
2. **Make every paragraph or section carry its own point, and cut restatement.** Value comes from sampling across units, and redundancy speeds the reader's drop-off. One new point per unit; no recaps of what an earlier unit already said.
3. **Keep units cheap to jump between.** For multi-part answers, use short headed sections or clearly separated paragraphs so a reader can leave a unit and land at the start of the next. Skimming gave no benefit when moving between units was costly.
