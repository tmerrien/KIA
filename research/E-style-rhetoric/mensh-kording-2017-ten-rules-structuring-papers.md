## Core claim (max 3 sentences)
A manuscript succeeds when readers can still state its one main contribution a year later. This is achieved by structure "at multiple scales—spanning the sentence, paragraph, section, and document," not by word choice. The default structure at every scale is context-content-conclusion (C-C-C): orient the reader, give the new material, then state what it means.

## Key evidence
- **Nature of evidence.** These are expert heuristics, written "focusing on how readers consume information". The article reports no experiment. It appeals to one psychology result: working-memory limits and the serial-position effect (Murdock 1968, ref. 5), where readers remember "the beginning and the end of a list" better than the middle. The authors "took our own advice" and say they gathered feedback from many colleagues. Treat the rules as codified practice, like Gopen & Swan.
- **Rule 1, the "Rule of One."** "Focus on a single message; papers that simultaneously focus on multiple contributions tend to be less convincing about each and are therefore less memorable." The title is "the ultimate refinement of the paper's contribution." The limit on simplification is explicit: make the claim "as simple as the data and logic can support but no simpler." The one contribution may be "multifaceted" if a bridge unifies the facets.
- **Rule 2, the curse of knowledge.** "Because you are the world's leading expert at exactly what you are doing, you are also the world's least qualified person to judge your writing from the perspective of the naïve reader. The majority of writing mistakes stem from this predicament." The prescriptions are to define technical terms, to "avoid abbreviations and acronyms so that readers do not have to go back," and to "minimize the number of loose threads that the reader has to keep in mind at any one time."
- **Rule 3, C-C-C, and the patient/impatient trade-off.** Missing context makes readers ask "Why was I told that?" Missing conclusion makes them ask "So what?" The authors acknowledge that "content first" (news style) serves the impatient reader. They choose C-C-C as the default only because a paper's title and abstract already deliver the content quickly. They warn that excessive content-first structure can "generate skepticism" when context that makes a claim credible is missing. At paragraph scale: "the first sentence defines the topic or context, the body hosts the novel content ..., and the last sentence provides the conclusion to be remembered". This is Gopen & Swan's topic/stress positions applied to the paragraph. Writers deviate from it by narrating chronologically, but readers "do not care about the chronological path by which you reached a result."
- **Rule 4, no zig-zag, and terminological constancy.** "Each subject should be covered in only one place in order to minimize the number of subject changes." Parallel messages get parallel syntax "so that this syntax becomes transparent to the reader." Precision, verbatim: "There is nothing wrong with using the same word multiple times ... Resist the temptation to use a different word to refer to the same concept—doing so makes readers wonder if the second word has a slightly different meaning."
- **Rules 5-8, structure by reader path.** The abstract is "for most readers, the only part of the paper that will be read," so it must carry the whole message. The most common abstract mistake "is to talk about results before the reader is ready to understand them." Results subsection headers and figure titles should be *declarative statements* of the conclusion ("the title of the figure should communicate the conclusion of the analysis, and the legend should explain how it was done"). Each results paragraph opens with its question and "ends with a sentence that answers the question." This makes a suspicious conclusion easy to locate and fact-check.
- **Rule 9, effort follows readership.** Readership falls off from title and abstract to figures, and the methods section is "read least of all. Budget accordingly." The recommended outline is "one informal sentence for each planned paragraph."
- **Table 1, a violation test per rule.** Each rule has an observable sign that it is being broken. Examples: Rule 1 is violated when "Readers cannot give 1-sentence summary"; Rule 3 when "Readers ask why something matters or what it means"; Rule 4 when "Readers stumble on a small section of the text."

## Relevance to AI answer style
- **Gopen & Swan scaled up.** Topic position and stress position become the first and last sentence of a paragraph, and the opening and closing of an answer. For an assistant, C-C-C maps to this order: a one-clause frame (what this answers), then the content, then the take-away. The authors justify their default by the reader's patience. A chat user asking a direct question is the *impatient* reader, so the answer should come first. The authors' own warning still applies: lead with the answer, but keep the context that makes it credible.
- **Precision versus compression.** Rule 4 says outright that repeating the exact term is correct and that elegant variation adds ambiguity. "Make it shorter" therefore never licenses swapping a term for a synonym or a new acronym. Rule 2 says to expand acronyms and define terms instead of compressing them. The Rule of One limits *scope* (one message), not *content* ("no simpler" than the data and logic support).
- **Declarative headers.** If an answer uses headers or a bolded lead line, they should state conclusions ("The cache is stale because X"), not topics ("Cache analysis"). A skimming reader then gets the content from the headers alone. This matches the F-pattern and layer-cake findings already in track F.
- **No chronological narration.** LLM agents tend to narrate their process ("First I checked..., then I found..."). Rule 3 names exactly this failure: readers want "the ultimate claim and the logic supporting it."
- **Self-check by summary.** Table 1's test applies to an answer: if its main point cannot be stated in one sentence, the answer is unfocused.

## Candidate rules for the harness
1. **One message, stated first, with its credibility context kept.** Every answer should have one statable main point, which appears in the first sentence. A multi-part answer needs one explicit bridge sentence that unifies the parts. Do not drop the context or caveat that makes the main point credible.
2. **Same concept, same term; no new acronyms.** Once a technical term is chosen, reuse it exactly. Never vary it for style. Do not coin abbreviations the user has not used. Define any term the user may not know, in the place where it first appears.
3. **Conclusions in headers, paragraph-last sentences, and lead lines; never narrate the process.** Headers and bolded leads state findings, not topics. Each paragraph ends on its answer. Report results and the logic that supports them, not the order in which the work was done.
