## Core claim (max 3 sentences)
Readers "make many of their most important interpretive decisions about the substance of prose based on clues they receive from its structure." So information "is interpreted more easily and more uniformly if it is placed where most readers expect to find it" (p. 550). The fix is to move information into the positions readers expect, not to shorten or simplify: "We have striven not for simplification but for clarification" (p. 551).

## Key evidence
- **Same data, different structure (p. 550).** A table of time/temperature readings, a run-on list of the same values, and a table with the columns reversed hold "precisely the same information". Yet readers find the context-left/new-right table easiest. This is a cleanly controlled demonstration of the idea, though it is not an experiment.
- **Length is explicitly not the variable (p. 553).** "Sheer length was neither the problem nor the solution. The revised version is not noticeably shorter than the original ... We have indeed deleted certain words, but not on the basis of wordiness or excess length." They reject readability formulas' 29-word limit: "We have seen 10-word sentences that are virtually impenetrable and ... 100-word sentences that flow effortlessly."
- **A testable definition of "too long" (p. 553).** "A sentence is too long when it has more viable candidates for stress positions than there are stress positions available." This makes sentence length a function of emphasis load, not word count.
- **Seven reader expectations, summarized on p. 558:**
  1. Follow a grammatical subject as soon as possible with its verb.
  2. Put the new information you want emphasized in the stress position.
  3. Put the person or thing whose story the sentence tells at the start, in the topic position.
  4. Put appropriate old information in the topic position, to link backward and give context forward.
  5. Articulate the action of every clause in its verb.
  6. Provide context before asking the reader to consider anything new.
  7. Make the relative emphases of the substance coincide with the emphasis the structure leads readers to expect.
- **Subject-verb separation (p. 552).** The URF example puts 23 words between subject and verb. "Anything of length that intervenes between subject and verb is read as an interruption, and therefore as something of lesser importance." The remedy is a choice: either promote the material to a stress position or delete it. "Only the author could tell us which." Deletion here is a judgment about relevance, not about word count.
- **Stress position = syntactic closure (p. 552).** The stress position "coincides with the moment of syntactic closure." A colon or semicolon creates a secondary stress position. "A whole list, numbered and indented, can occupy the stress position of a sentence if it has been clearly announced as being all that remains of that sentence." This directly licenses introduce-then-list formatting.
- **Misplaced stress has two failure modes (p. 552).** Either nothing emphasis-worthy sits at the end, so readers must guess, or an "imposter" sits there and gets the emphasis.
- **Topic position (p. 554).** "Readers expect a unit of discourse to be a story about whoever shows up first." "Pollen is dispersed by bees" is the better sentence in a paragraph about pollen, so the passive voice is defended when it serves the topic. In the earthquake paragraph, the recurring old information ("recurrence intervals") "rarely appears at the beginning of sentences." Gopen and Swan call misplaced old/new information "the No. 1 problem in American professional writing today" (p. 555). They attribute it to writers rushing to record the new thought first.
- **Principle, not rule (p. 556).** "Put the old information in the topic position and the new information in the stress position" is rejected as an over-simplification. The correct form is: "Put in the topic position the old information that links backward; put in the stress position the new information you want the reader to emphasize." Also (p. 558): "None of these reader-expectation principles should be considered 'rules.' Slavish adherence to them will succeed no better than has slavish adherence to avoiding split infinitives or to using the active voice."
- **Structure reveals logical gaps, and the fix adds words (pp. 556-558).** In the dG/dC enthalpy passage, restructuring exposes a missing causal link between the derivatization and the measurement. The repair adds two sentences ("Consequently, when the derivatized nucleosides are dissolved ..."). "Filling the gaps required the addition of extra material" (p. 558). The gap happens when "the connections are so clear in the writer's mind that they seem unnecessary to state" (p. 556), which is the curse of knowledge.
- **Actions in verbs (p. 557).** In the TFIIIA passage the verbs are "is ... is ... are presumed to be ... has", while the real actions ("limit", "inhibit") hide elsewhere. "If the actions are not to be found in the verbs, then we as readers have no secondary structural clues for where to locate them."
- **Nature of the evidence.** Every example is an expert worked revision of real published or accepted passages. There is no reader experiment. The authors frame their claims as probabilistic: "we can only increase the odds that a large majority of readers will tend to interpret our discourse according to our intentions" (p. 553). Treat the claims as codified heuristics. For an empirical basis, look to given-new psycholinguistics (see proposals).

## Relevance to AI answer style
- **Precision versus compression, stated outright.** The authors claim clarity "without oversimplifying" and say "we have neither decreased the jargon nor diluted the science." A precise technical term stays. What changes is where it sits: introduced in a stress position, then reused as old information in the topic position. Shortening is not the goal. Two of their four revisions got longer because restructuring exposed a missing causal link. For an assistant, "concise" must never mean deleting the *because* that links two facts.
- **A sentence-length budget defined by emphasis, not words.** Their "too long" test gives a harness check: count the new, emphasis-worthy facts in a sentence. If there are more of them than stress positions (sentence end, plus one per semicolon/colon or per announced list item), split the sentence or make a list.
- **Lists are licensed structurally.** An announced list can occupy one stress position, and each item has its own. That is a principled reason to break multi-fact answers into an introduced list. It is not a scannability fad.
- **Subject-verb gaps.** LLM output often packs parentheticals and qualifiers between subject and verb ("The function, which was added in v2 and is used by the CLI and the server, returns ..."). Readers discount that material as an interruption. Either promote it or cut it.
- **Topic consistency.** Readers take each sentence as being about "whoever shows up first." An answer that switches topic subjects every sentence (tool, then user, then file, then error) reads as several stories. Keeping one topic string (for example, "the config") across a paragraph is cheap and improves uniform interpretation. Eisenhart's LLM tests (already in this track) found exactly this skill weak in ChatGPT.
- **Don't end on lackluster material.** Ending a sentence on hedges, citations or "as mentioned above" wastes the stress position.

## Candidate rules for the harness
1. **Emphasis budget per sentence.** Each sentence should carry at most one new, emphasis-worthy fact per stress position: the sentence end, plus one after each semicolon or colon, or one per item in an announced list. Put that fact last. Never end a sentence on a hedge, a source tag, or metadiscourse.
2. **Old first, link backward; keep one topic string.** Open each sentence with information the user already has: their terms, or the previous sentence's new item. Keep the same main entity as subject across a paragraph unless the story really changes. Use the passive voice if that keeps the topic in place.
3. **Keep subject and verb adjacent, and keep the connectives.** Do not insert more than a short phrase between a subject and its verb. Either promote the interrupting material to its own clause or delete it if it is irrelevant. When cutting words, never delete the causal or logical link (because, so, therefore, which means) between two stated facts. If a link is missing, add it, even though the answer gets longer.
