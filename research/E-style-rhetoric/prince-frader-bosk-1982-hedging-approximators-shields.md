## Core claim
"Hedge" covers two different operations:
- **Approximators** change the proposition itself. They fall into two kinds:
  - **adaptors** ("sort of blue") adapt a term to a nonprototypical case;
  - **rounders** ("about 3.2 kg") mark a figure as rounded.
- **Shields** leave the proposition intact and mark the speaker's commitment to it. They also fall into two kinds:
  - **plausibility shields** ("I think") mark belief reached by plausible rather than deductive reasoning;
  - **attribution shields** ("according to her estimates") mark that the belief comes from someone else.

The ICU corpus shows that shields are not clichés. They are added in self-repairs, mostly to assertions, and are understood in context. The authors conclude that marking such beliefs "demonstrates a scholarly orderliness in their representation of knowledge" (p. 25).

## Key evidence
**Corpus (pp. 2-3)**
- Morning rounds in a pediatric ICU were audio-taped between August 1978 and January 1979.
- Twelve randomly chosen hours were transcribed narrowly.
- Using G. Lakoff's (1972) definition, "a word or phrase whose job it is to make things fuzzier", the corpus has **150-450 hedges per hour, "more than one every fifteen seconds"** (p. 3).

**Approximators vs shields (pp. 4-6)**
- The contrast is shown with "His feet were blue" / "sort of blue" / "I think his feet were blue".
- Plain "His feet were blue" enters the context under K (*the speaker knows that*).
- "Sort of blue" also enters under K. It is a *different* proposition, nonprototypically blue, held with *full* commitment.
- "I think…" keeps the original proposition but enters under P (*for all the speaker knows*).
- So approximators belong to semantics and are truth-conditional. Example: "A chicken is a sort of a bird" is TRUE, while "A robin is a sort of a bird" is FALSE (pp. 6-7).
- Shields belong to pragmatics: they implicate a marked commitment.

**Adaptors are a lexical-precision device (pp. 8-9).** When reality does not match the prototype of an available term, a speaker can:
- (A) use an exact but irrelevant term ("set" for crusted, "mauve" for "a little bit on the blue side"). This never occurs in the corpus, because the discourse is goal-directed;
- (B) coin a new term. This is "even more unsatisfactory": it "would convey nothing to the coparticipants" because diagnosis runs on "a knowledge-store encoded in old terms";
- (C) use the closest term and ignore the mismatch. This is acceptable unless the mismatch may matter;
- (D) use the closest relevant term and mark the mismatch. This is "the most reasonable choice", and it is what adaptors do.

Footnote 1 gives a related case from Tannen (1980). The physician's lexicon for a newborn's color has *yellow* and *white* but not the parent's word *pale*, and the dialogue fails.

**Rounders are shorthand, not doubt (p. 10).** "Precise numbers do exist," but the precise term "is not relevant… or is not immediately available," or the speaker means a range.
- Example: two monitoring systems give slightly different blood-pressure figures, so "about … between forty and fifty".
- Rounders are "extremely common… in the domain of measurements" (p. 10).
- They never appear in self-repairs that add a hedge, and are "a shorthand device" (p. 22).

**Shields (pp. 11-19)**
- **Plausibility shields** ("I think", "probably", "as far as I can tell right now", "consistent with") are typical of planning and diagnosing (p. 16).
- **Attribution shields** ("mother says that", "according to her estimates", "presumably", "documented") are typical of background history (p. 19).

**Gricean derivation of attribution shields (pp. 17-18).**
- A bare assertion invokes Quality (ii), "adequate evidence", so it is equivalent to the redundant "According to me…".
- "According to Dr. Smith" adds material. By Quantity, the hearer infers "Dr. Smith and not necessarily me".
- This forms a scale with the speaker at the top. How much the speaker commits depends on the attributee's rank: physicians rank above nurses, and nurses above parents.
- In the corpus, the hedged claim attributed to the mother is the one later explicitly questioned (ex. 17, p. 19).

**Against numerical replacement (pp. 15-16, 19, note 2).**
- Bryant & Norman (1979) found that physicians' rankings of 30 such expressions were inconsistent. "A radiologist used 'consistent with' when certain."
- Prince et al. answer that in context, among a tight speech community, shields were "immediately and appropriately understood", and they "find no evidence for the supposition that Shields impede communication".
- Note 2 (p. 26): numerical ratings "would probably serve only to give the physicians one more thing to be uncertain about and, ironically, to hedge."
- Note that this claim rests on the authors' qualitative reading of the transcripts, not on a comprehension test.

**Self-repair data: hedges are not clichés (pp. 21-24)**

*Rate of self-repairs.* In one two-hour session there are 97 substantive self-repairs, one every 84 s.

*Share that add a hedge.* 38 of the 97 (39%) add a hedge:

| Hedge added | Self-repairs |
|---|---|
| Plausibility shield | 19 (50%) |
| Attribution shield | 12 (32%) |
| Adaptor | 7 (18%) |
| Rounder | 0 |

*Assertions vs questions.* 95% of the hedge-adding repairs occur in assertions, against 83% of other repairs (p<.1).

*Share of each hedge type that appears in a self-repair:*

| Hedge type | Share in self-repairs |
|---|---|
| Shields (all) | 10% |
| Approximators (all) | 4% (p<.02 vs shields) |
| Attribution shields | 21% |
| Plausibility shields | 8% |
| Adaptors | 4% |
| Rounders | 0% |

Attribution shields differ from each of the other three types, with p from <.02 to <.001.

*The authors' reading.* "A major source of dysfluency… is the felt need… to hedge some proposition… already begun… in an unhedged… form, in particular, to implicitly disclaim personal commitment by explicitly attributing the belief to another" (p. 24).

**Face (p. 24 and notes 3-4)**
- Hedging is linked to "a large stake in saving their professional face".
- Note 3: Shuy reports that lawyers' hedging fades as they near the courtroom.
- Note 4: Akita reports few hedges in physician-*patient* talk. Both are personal communications, not data.

## Relevance to AI answer style
1. **"Remove hedges" is the wrong rule. Remove the wrong *kind* of hedge.** Thomas & Turner, and Pinker after them, ban process, liability and worth hedges. Prince et al. show that a hedge class can be content:
   - an **approximator** changes truth conditions. Deleting "about" or "sort of" asserts a different and *false* proposition. That is lossy compression, not concision;
   - a **plausibility shield** reports the epistemic route (inferred rather than observed);
   - an **attribution shield** reports the source and lets the reader weight it by the source's credibility.

   None of these is a tic when used selectively. Uniform shields on every sentence carry no information, because they no longer distinguish anything.
2. **Option (D) is the precision procedure.** When reality misses the prototype:
   - do not reach for an exact but irrelevant word (A);
   - do not coin a term the reader does not share (B);
   - take the reader's established term and mark the deviation with an adaptor.

   This settles the precision/compression question for terminology: the "exact term" must be exact *and shared*. When none fits, closest-shared-term plus adaptor beats both a private neologism and silent approximation (C), provided the mismatch matters.
3. **Rounders are legitimate compression.** "About 3.2 kg" drops digits that do not bear on the goal and *says so*. Rounding with the marker is precision-preserving. Rounding without it ("3 kg") silently changes the claim.
4. **Attribution beats doubt-words.** "The docs say X" or "per the 2023 changelog" tells the reader *why* commitment is limited and how to check, which "I think X" does not. Attribution shields were the hedges speakers most often went back to insert (21% of their occurrences were in self-repairs). In expert talk, sourcing is the commitment marker people care most about getting right.
5. **Context calibrates verbal hedges; isolation does not.** Prince et al. and Bryant & Norman disagree on whether verbal probability terms communicate. That disagreement is between in-context reading among peers and out-of-context ratings. An assistant talks to strangers, not to a tight speech community, so its shields lack that shared calibration. This argues for a fixed, consistent mapping from words to confidence (cf. Kim 2024 and Steyvers 2025 in this track) or for a number where one exists. The paper's own data do not test this. It is an inference.

## Candidate rules for the harness
1. **Keep approximators, cut tics.** Never delete "about", "roughly", "a kind of" or "nearly" when it marks that a figure is rounded or that a term fits only approximately. Deleting it asserts a false, sharper claim. Delete hedges that mark nothing (blanket "may", "it seems", "I think" on facts the assistant is sure of).
2. **Name the source instead of a doubt-word.** When commitment is limited because the claim comes from elsewhere, attribute it ("the README says…", "per RFC 9110…"). Do not use "I believe", "probably" or "it seems". Use a plausibility shield ("likely, because…") only for claims the assistant *inferred*, and state the basis in the same clause.
3. **Closest shared term, plus a marker.** If no established term fits the case exactly, use the reader's nearest standard term with an explicit adaptor ("effectively a race condition", "a cache, in the loose sense"). Do not coin a private label, and do not silently use the nearest term when the mismatch would change the reader's action.
