## Core claim (max 3 sentences)
If two English paragraphs hold the same propositions but one puts old information in sentence topics and new information in comments, the old-first one is read faster, judged more readable, and remembered better. Two kinds of old-first paragraph both beat their reversed variants: "topically linked" paragraphs, which keep a constant topic, and "rhetorically linked" ones, where each comment becomes the next topic. Readers seem to "add new information to an anchor of old information in memory", which extends Haviland & Clark's given-new strategy from sentence pairs to whole paragraphs.

## Key evidence
- **Same content, reordered.** Each test pair had "the same number of topics and the same propositional information"; only the old/new order was reversed (pp. 4-5). To head off a style confound, colleagues flagged any awkward word or sentence and it was revised (p. 5).
- **Two kinds of link, both tested.** In a *topically linked* paragraph the topic stays the same ("the Marathon ... Its weight ... its two-inch width ... Its most unique characteristic ..."). In a *rhetorically linked* paragraph the comment chains into the next topic ("Epic poems usually include a long narrative or story. This story is almost always marked by certain conventions. One of these is the epic simile ..."). Variants put new information first ("A mere two pounds is its weight"). These correspond to Daneš's constant-theme and simple-linear progressions.
- **Eight measures.** Five readability measures: subjective judgments under three alerting conditions, timed oral reading, and five-minute typing tests. Three retention measures: double-distractor recognition with new-information targets, short-answer recall of new information, and immediate free recall scored for function words, content words and three-word sequences (p. 6).
- **Result.** "In almost every experiment particular topically linked and rhetorically linked paragraphs tested out as statistically highly superior to their variants." The only exceptions were non-significant advantages for variants in typing accuracy and in recall of function words (topical), and in typing speed and short-answer correct answers (rhetorical) (pp. 6-7). The paper gives no test statistics.
- **Readers repair reversed order themselves (p. 8).** In free recall of the reversed topical variant, 60 of 101 grammatical sentences put the old information back in the topic; 25 of 36 subjects did so at least once. For the reversed rhetorical variant the figures were 24 of 126 sentences and 14 of 36 subjects. This is the strongest single piece of evidence: memory itself reorganizes text into old-then-new.
- **Readers can say why.** Subjects' written justifications included "the main idea comes first, followed by an explanation of the idea", "lets you know right away what it is about", and "the end of a sentence leads right into the beginning of the next" (pp. 9-10).
- **Limits.** These were short constructed paragraphs, not natural texts. The paper reports no statistics. Truth-conditional equivalence of the pairs is asserted, with Sachs (1967) cited as support. A link between preferring linked forms and reading-comprehension scores is called "preliminary" (p. 9).

## Relevance to AI answer style
- **Empirical support for the topic/stress rules.** The Gopen & Swan rules in this track are expert heuristics. This paper shows that the same old-before-new ordering, with *no change in content or length*, measurably helps reading speed and recall. It is the cleanest evidence in the crawl that density can rise without losing information: the same propositions are processed more cheaply.
- **Precision versus compression.** Nothing was deleted between conditions. The gain comes entirely from *where* the precise term sits. Repeating the key term (or a pronoun or synonym) in topic position is what readers use as the "pointer" to the memory anchor. For an assistant, a word-count-driven style that cuts repeated anchor terms ("it", "this" with an unclear referent, or dropping the subject) can delete the very cue that makes the next sentence cheap.
- **Two legitimate paragraph shapes.** Use a *constant topic* for describing one thing (an API, a config option): the same subject across sentences. Use a *chain* (the last item of one sentence opens the next) for causal or step-by-step explanations. Both are fine. The failure is a new-first sentence with the anchor buried at the end.
- **Readers reorder anyway.** Readers who saw reversed text rewrote it old-first in recall. Reversed order makes the reader do work the writer could have done.

## Candidate rules for the harness
1. **Anchor first.** Begin each sentence after the first with something the user already has: their own term, the question's subject, or the previous sentence's last new item. Put the new fact at the end. Do not reverse this to "front-load" the novelty ("A 30-second timeout is what fixes it" -> "The fix is a 30-second timeout").
2. **Pick a paragraph shape and hold it.** When describing one entity, keep it as the grammatical subject throughout (constant topic). When explaining a causal or procedural sequence, chain the end of each sentence into the start of the next. Do not change the topic every sentence without a reason.
3. **Keep the anchor term; do not compress it away.** When trimming, never drop the repeated key term or turn it into an ambiguous "it/this/that" if the antecedent is more than one sentence back. A repeated precise term is not redundancy. It is the pointer readers use to attach new information.
