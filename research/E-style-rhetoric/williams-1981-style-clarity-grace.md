## Core claim (max 3 sentences)

Readers judge prose "clear" or "turgid" by how its grammar is arranged, and the decisive arrangement is whether the subjects name the main characters and the verbs name their crucial actions. At the passage level the same holds: sentences should begin with old, familiar information and end, in the stress position, with new, complex, or emphatic information. Concision follows from these choices: "What counts is not the number of words in a sentence, but how easily we get from beginning to end while understanding everything in between" (Michigan Bar Journal, Jan. 1992).

## Key evidence (what the text actually offers)

- **The two-level model.** A sentence has a fixed grammatical level (Subject + Verb + Complement) and a variable story level (Characters + Actions). "To a significant degree, we judge a style to be clear or unclear according to how consistently a writer aligns those two levels" (MBJ Feb. 1992).
- **Feelings versus features.** Words like *turgid* and *awkward* "do not describe sentences on the page; we describe how we feel about them" (MBJ Jan. 1992; same move in *Basics* p. 7). The book's contribution is a second vocabulary of diagnosable features.
- **Mechanical diagnostics (testable, unlike "omit needless words"):**
  1. Underline the first 7-8 words of each sentence, ignoring short introductory phrases. Flag the sentence if the simple subject is an abstract noun, or if it takes more than 7-8 words to reach the verb (*Basics* p. 13; MBJ Jan. 1992 says 6-7).
  2. Find the characters hidden in possessives, *of*/*by* phrases, or adjectives (*governmental* → government). Find the actions hidden in nominalizations (-tion, -ment, -ence). Ask "Who is doing what?" (*Basics* pp. 21-22).
  3. Five spot patterns: a nominalization as subject of an empty verb (*The intention of the committee is to audit*); a nominalization after an empty verb (*conducted an investigation*); one after *there is*/*there are*; two nominalizations linked by *was a result of*; and strings of nominalizations joined by *of* (*Basics* pp. 14-16).
- **Consequences of de-nominalizing (*Basics* pp. 16-17; MBJ Jan. 1992).** Prose becomes more concrete. It drops articles and prepositions. The logic is spelled out with *because*, *although* and *if* instead of fuzzy *of*/*by*. The order of events can follow real chronology. The examples use numbered events to show nominalizations scrambling sequence.
- **Character deletion destroys meaning, not just style.** "A decision was made in favor of doing a study of the disagreements" can mean "We decided that I should study why they disagreed" or other things. "Who fears? Who recommends? ... if the context is ambiguous, you risk them guessing wrongly" (*Basics* pp. 20-23). "The first principle of a clear style is this: make the subjects of most of your verbs the main characters in your story" (p. 21).
- **Useful nominalizations are kept (*Basics* p. 18; MBJ Feb. 1992):**
  - a short subject that refers back to the previous sentence (*This decision*), which builds cohesion;
  - a noun naming the object of the verb (*her request*);
  - a concept so familiar it acts as a "virtual actor" (*taxation without representation*);
  - ideas that exist only as nouns (*freedom, death, love*).
  "You must develop an eye for distinguishing nominalizations expressing familiar ideas from those that you can revise into verbs."
- **Old before new / cohesion (*Basics* pp. 34-37).** "Sentences are cohesive when the last few words of one set up information that appears in the first few words of the next." The rules are "Begin sentences with information familiar to your readers" and "End sentences with information readers cannot predict." Williams explains the passive's reason to exist as a way to put old information first (the black-hole example, pp. 35-36). He also warns: "after you've worked on your own ideas for a while, they all seem familiar—to you" (p. 37).
- **Stress / emphasis (*Basics* pp. 44-49).** The last few words of a sentence receive special emphasis (the "stress" position). "Your sentences should begin with elements that are relatively short ... followed by a short, concrete subject, followed by a verb expressing a specific action". The general principle is "to carry the reader not from complexity to simplicity, but from simplicity to complexity" (p. 47). New technical terms belong at the end of the sentence, even introduced through metadiscourse: "*We term these cells* lymphokine-activated killer (LAK) cells" (p. 47). The tactics are to trim the end, shift peripheral ideas left, avoid "anticlimactic metadiscourse" (*it must be remembered*), and shift new information right (pp. 48-49). The diagnostic is to read aloud and "tap your finger hard" on the last three or four words.
- **Concision (*Basics* pp. 82-87).** Six principles:
  1. Delete meaningless words (*actually, basically, certain, really*).
  2. Delete doubled words (*full and complete*).
  3. Delete what readers can infer: redundant modifiers (*future plans*), redundant categories (*pink in color*), general implications.
  4. Replace a phrase with a word.
  5. Change negatives to affirmatives.
  6. Delete useless adjectives and adverbs.
  Principle 4 is explicitly about precision, not deletion: it "is especially difficult to fix, because you need a big vocabulary and the wit to use it" ("carefully read what you have written → edit"; "nouns instead of verbs → nominalizations"). Concision is placed *after* characters/actions, cohesion and emphasis: a sentence can satisfy those and still be wordy (p. 82).
- **Nature of the evidence.** Nearly all of it is paired-sentence demonstrations ("Most readers think (2b) tells its story more clearly than (2a)"). The pages read cite no controlled experiments. The MBJ editor's note calls the book "grounded in research", but the experimental basis is **UNVERIFIED** from what was read. Treat the principles as expert-codified heuristics with strong face validity, not as measured effects.

## Relevance to AI answer style

- **Precision versus compression, made operational.** Williams separates three things:
  - Deleting words that carry no meaning (fine).
  - Replacing a phrase with the one exact word (precision; it needs vocabulary).
  - Deleting *characters* or burying *actions* in nouns (compression that loses meaning). This makes text shorter-looking but more ambiguous: "Who fears? Who recommends?"

  An assistant trimming answers with noun-stacks ("Config validation failure prevention requires schema enforcement") commits exactly the compression Williams diagnoses. The fix ("If you enforce a schema, the config will fail validation earlier") is often the same length or shorter, and it is unambiguous about who does what.
- **Answer-first is a stress/topic decision.** Opening with a short known topic (the user's question terms) and putting the new payload at the end of the sentence matches how readers allocate attention. The "complexity → simplicity" warning applies to opening sentences that front-load jargon.
- **Technical terms:** introduce a new term at the end of a sentence, after its gloss. Then reuse it as a short, familiar subject. This is the "useful nominalization" that links sentences. A precise term earns its place once it is familiar.
- **The curse of knowledge:** "after you've worked on your own ideas for a while, they all seem familiar—to you." An assistant deep in a task context treats its own intermediate labels as old information. The user does not share them.
- **LLM-specific gap (Eisenhart):** models already over-perform on deleting words and under-perform on character/action alignment and topic strings. So harness rules should target structure, not more deletion.

## Candidate rules for the harness

1. **Who-does-what check.** In each sentence that states an action, the grammatical subject should name the actor (user, tool, function, system), and the verb should name the action. Rewrite subjects that are abstract nominalizations (*-tion/-ment/-ence* + empty verb such as *is/occurs/was performed/conduct*) into actor + verb form. Never drop an actor to save words if the reader could then guess the wrong one.
2. **Old → new, payload last.** Start each sentence with something the user already has (their terms, the previous sentence's last idea). End with the new fact, number, or term. Do not finish on hedges or metadiscourse ("..., it should be noted"). Introduce a new technical term at the end of a sentence, right after its gloss. After that, it may serve as a short subject.
3. **Cut zero-content words, keep exact words.** Delete meaningless intensifiers, doubled pairs, and implied categories ("in color", "future plans"). Prefer replacing a multi-word phrase with the single exact term over deleting content. Prefer affirmatives over stacked negatives. Never pass a shorter but nominalized rewrite as "more concise".
