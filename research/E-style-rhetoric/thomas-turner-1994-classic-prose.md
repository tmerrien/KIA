## Core claim
Style is not a list of surface techniques. It is a **stand** on a few basic questions: truth, presentation, scene, cast (writer and reader), and thought and language. Each coherent set of answers defines a different style. In **classic style**:
- the motive is truth and the purpose is presentation;
- the reader is an intellectual equal, and the occasion is a conversation;
- "prose is a window": the writer puts the reader where they can *see* the thing, rather than arguing for it.

## Key evidence (as summarized)
The method is argument by contrast between exemplary passages. La Rochefoucauld's portrait of Madame de Chevreuse, the prototype of classic style, is set against a periodic sentence from Samuel Johnson. The classic sentence "has a clear direction and goal," and it orders the truth independently of how the writer found it. Johnson's sentence "does not telegraph its structure from the opening."

**Presentation**
- Classic prose is "a perfectly efficient instrument, which neither invents nor distorts."
- It is "perfect performance," so it excludes three hedges:
  - **process:** hesitations from being mid-thought;
  - **liability:** insurance against having overlooked something;
  - **worth:** justifying the project.
- **"Every word counts."** Classic style "allows browsing but not skimming," because "essential nuances … are lost in skimming."
- **"Clarity everywhere is not accuracy everywhere."** When accuracy needs "complicated qualifications of no consequence to the main issue, classic writers do not hesitate to simplify."

**Scene**
- The model is one person speaking to another, and the prose should be readable aloud correctly the first time.
- Classic style eschews metadiscourse ("as we shall see," "three paragraphs ago").
- Prose is "efficient but not rushed."

**Cast**
- The reader is competent and "could take the next turn in the conversation."
- The writer "does not persuade; he presents."
- The writer "does all the work invisibly." The book's image is the restaurant: the kitchen is hidden, the dining room serene.

**Thought and language**
- Language is sufficient, and there are two kinds of fit between thought and expression:
  - **lexical:** "there are already expressions in the language to fit any thought";
  - **structural:** the same image schema structures both the thought and the sentence.
- **Source-path-goal schema.** This gives *stress position*: the end of the sentence carries what matters most.
- **Focus-then-inspect schema.** Present the subject first, then its details.
- **Action schema.** The grammatical subject is the agent and the verb is the action.
- **Three ways prose misaligns with thought:**
  - no image schema at all;
  - a schema that is started but not completed;
  - mismatched schemas.
- **Abstractions are not bad in themselves.** They can be "exhaustively definite" and stated in "direct and simple language."

**Other styles defined by contrast:** plain, reflexive, practical, contemplative, romantic, prophetic and oratorical.
- **Practical style:** "the best presentation will allow the reader to acquire timely information with a minimum of distraction." In classic style, by contrast, "neither writer nor reader has a job."
- **Plain vs classic:** "The truth is pure and simple" is plain style. "The truth is rarely pure, and never simple" is classic: it keeps the plain claim and adds a refinement.

**"Trade secrets."** The authors concede two limits:
- Classic style tacitly limits its domain to truths that "can be presented briefly and memorably."
- No truth is really mind-independent. The style rests on enabling conventions, not facts.

## Relevance to AI answer style
1. **An assistant works in the practical scene but should take the classic stance.** By Thomas & Turner's own taxonomy, an AI answer serves a reader with a job, who needs "timely information with a minimum of distraction." That is *practical* style, not classic. Pinker's popularization blurs this distinction. The workable synthesis is:
   - from the practical scene, take scannability and order by the reader's need;
   - from the classic stance, take presenting rather than arguing, the reader as an equal, no visible kitchen and no metadiscourse.
2. **"Every word counts" is precision, not compression.** The ideal is prose with no dead words, in which each surviving word carries a nuance that skimming would lose. This is the opposite of telegraphic compression, which removes the nuance along with the filler.
3. **Lexical fit is the precision principle.** The claim that "there are already expressions in the language to fit any thought" licenses searching for the exact term instead of a periphrasis. One right word replaces a clause and loses nothing.
4. **Selective simplification is principled.** "Clarity everywhere is not accuracy everywhere" licenses dropping qualifications that do not bear on the main issue. Qualifications that change the answer must stay. This is the line between legitimate omission and lossy compression.
5. **Hedge taxonomy for diagnosis.** Hedges of process ("let me think…"), liability (blanket "this may not be accurate") and worth ("great question") are all removable. The trade-secret caveat, together with Nardo's critique, implies that a *specific, calibrated* uncertainty is content, not a hedge.
6. **Image schemas give testable ordering rules.**
   - Focus-then-inspect: name the subject or answer, then the details.
   - Stress position: put the key new item at the end of the sentence.
   - Agent-as-subject for actions.

## Candidate rules for the harness
1. **Present, don't narrate.** Give the result as already thought out. Show no process ("Let me think…", "First I'll look at…"), no worth-hedges ("Great question"), no liability boilerplate, and no metadiscourse ("As mentioned above", "In this answer"). The kitchen stays out of the dining room. The exception: show reasoning when the user asked for it or must verify it.
2. **Find the exact word before cutting words.** When a phrase circles an idea, replace it with the established term for that idea (lexical fit). Then cut only qualifications that would not change the user's decision. Keep every qualification that would.
3. **Focus, then inspect.** Each answer and each paragraph opens by naming its subject or conclusion, then gives details in order. Within a sentence, put the decisive new information last (stress position), and make the actor the grammatical subject of any action.
