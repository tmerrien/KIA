## Core claim
Good expository prose follows **classic style**. In classic style, "prose is a window onto the world": a writer and reader who are "equals" converse, and the writer "orients the reader so she can see for herself." The chief cause of opaque writing is the **curse of knowledge**, "a difficulty in imagining what it is like for someone else not to know something that you know." Lists of rules (Strunk & White, Orwell) cannot replace this stance, because they rest on a weak grasp of grammar and sometimes contradict themselves.

## Key evidence
**Classic style.** The model is taken from Thomas & Turner (1994):
- "The guiding metaphor of classic style is seeing the world."
- Classic style is contrasted with other styles. In the **practical** style, "the writer's goal is to satisfy a reader's need for a particular kind of information." In the **self-conscious/postmodern** style, the writer's concern is "to escape being convicted of philosophical naïveté." That defensiveness, together with the curse of knowledge, produces academese.
- In the interview: "Classic style makes writing, which is necessarily artificial, as artificially natural as possible."

**Symptoms diagnosed (Chronicle essay):**
- **Compulsive hedging.** Writers "mindlessly cushion their prose with wads of fluff." Pinker does not ban hedges: "It's not that good writers never hedge their claims. It's that their hedging is a choice, not a tic."
- **Metadiscourse.** "Verbiage about verbiage" is "there to help the writer, not the reader." The rule: "The art of classic prose is to use signposts sparingly."
- **Nominalization.** It "takes a perfectly spry verb and embalms it into a lifeless noun." These are Helen Sword's "zombie nouns."
- **Metaconcepts.** Vacuous abstractions are "concepts about concepts." Writers come to use them as "containers in which they store and handle all their ideas."

**Curse of knowledge: cognitive basis.**
- **Developmental false-belief findings.** The cited studies are Wimmer & Perner (1983) and a candy-box/pencils paradigm. Children "simply can't reconstruct their prior ignorance" (APS report).
- **Adults show the same bias.** "Even adults slightly tilt their guess about where a person will look for a hidden object" (Chronicle).
- **Experts' chunking.** "The mind can package ideas into bigger and bigger units." Experts forget that readers lack those chunks, so their jargon and abbreviations go unexplained.
- **Cost of abbreviations.** Saving the writer keystrokes can "come at the cost of many minutes stolen from their readers."
- **Glossing a term costs little.** Writing "Arabidopsis, a flowering mustard plant" will "use up no more space on the page and be no less scientific."
- **Use examples.** Readers "thank a writer for the copious use of *for example, as in,* and *such as*."
- **Remedies.** Empathy alone fails, because people are poor at guessing what others know (APS). The fix is empirical: "showing a draft to a sample of real readers and seeing if they can follow it." Pinker also recommends rereading after a delay. That recommendation comes from secondary summaries of the WSJ essay and is UNVERIFIED.

**Critique of rule lists (Prologue, quoted via Wax 2014):**
- "Strunk and White, for all their intuitive feel for style, had a tenuous grasp of grammar. They misdefined terms such as phrase, participle, and relative clause, and in steering their readers away from passive verbs ... they botched their examples of both."
- Their sentence "Many a tame sentence ... can be made lively and emphatic by substituting a transitive in the active voice" "uses the passive voice to warn against the passive voice."
- "George Orwell, in his vaunted 'Politics and the English Language,' fell into the same trap."
- "Telling writers to avoid the passive is bad advice," because the passive "has a number of indispensable functions because of the way it engages a reader's attention and memory."

UNVERIFIED: the exact wording of Pinker's comments on "omit needless words" itself. A 2014 review (McGrath, via search summary only) says the book has no single maxim to rival it.

## Relevance to AI answer style
Pinker gives a cognitive account of *why* concise answers help, and it is not "fewer words." The reader has a limited working memory and lacks the writer's chunks. Every token should therefore either point at the thing in the world or supply a chunk the reader is missing.

This separates PRECISION from COMPRESSION:
- **Compression is the curse of knowledge in action.** Abbreviations, unglossed jargon and skipped steps save the writer's words and spend the reader's minutes.
- **Precision is classic style.** It uses the exact term, plus a short gloss when the reader may lack it ("Arabidopsis, a flowering mustard plant"). That costs "no more space" and keeps the full meaning.
- **Padding goes, information stays.** Cut metadiscourse ("In this answer I will..."), reflexive hedges, zombie nouns ("perform an investigation of") and metaconcepts ("approach," "framework," "level"). Keep glosses, examples and scoped qualifiers.
- **Rule lists fail.** Pinker's critique of Strunk and Orwell warns against harness rules like "never use the passive." The passive keeps the topic in subject position and orders given-before-new information.
- **Calibrate confidence (Nardo's critique).** An assistant must still state confidence. Classic style's "hedge by choice, not tic" should be read as "calibrated, local qualifiers," not as "assert everything flatly."

## Candidate rules for the harness
1. **Point at the thing, not at the answer.** Open with the fact, step or result itself. Use no metadiscourse ("Here's an overview...", "In this response I will...") and at most one signpost in a long answer. Prefer verbs to zombie nouns ("investigate," not "conduct an investigation of").
2. **Curse-of-knowledge check on terms.** Do not abbreviate any term the user has not used. When an exact technical term is the right word, keep it and add a gloss of a few words or one "for example" the first time it appears. Never drop the term to save words, and never leave it unexplained for a reader who may lack it.
3. **Hedge once, where it applies.** Attach a qualifier only to the specific claim that is uncertain, with a reason or a confidence level ("likely, because X"). Delete blanket softeners ("generally," "it may be worth noting"). Do not suppress real uncertainty either, since Nardo's critique applies.
