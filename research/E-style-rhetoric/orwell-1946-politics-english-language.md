# Orwell (1946), "Politics and the English Language"

## Citation
Orwell, George (1946). "Politics and the English Language." *Horizon* 13(76), April 1946. (Volume/issue numbers: UNVERIFIED. The month and venue are verified on the Orwell Foundation page.)
Primary text read: The Orwell Foundation (reproduced with permission of the Orwell Estate): https://www.orwellfoundation.com/the-orwell-foundation/orwell/essays-and-other-works/politics-and-the-english-language/

## Core claim
Modern prose is made of "ready-made phrases" chosen before the meaning, "tacked together like the sections of a prefabricated hen-house". As a result, "the slovenliness of our language makes it easier for us to have foolish thoughts." The remedy is "to let the meaning choose the word, and not the other way about." Orwell treats vague, inflated diction as a cause of bad thinking and a cover for evasion: "The great enemy of clear language is insincerity."

## Key evidence
This is an argumentative essay built on quoted specimens of contemporary prose, a taxonomy of faults, and a parody. It reports no empirical data.
- Taxonomy of "swindles and perversions":
  - **Dying metaphors**: "a huge dump of worn-out metaphors which have lost all evocative power".
  - **Operators or verbal false limbs**: they "save the trouble of picking out appropriate verbs and nouns". Examples: "render inoperative, militate against, prove unacceptable, make contact with".
  - **Pretentious diction**: words that "dress up simple statements and give an air of scientific impartiality to biassed judgements".
  - **Meaningless words**: words that "not only do not point to any discoverable object" but are hardly expected to.
- Parody: Orwell quotes Ecclesiastes ("I returned and saw under the sun, that the race is not to the swift, nor the battle to the strong ...") and then rewrites it in modern abstract prose ("Objective consideration of contemporary phenomena compels the conclusion ..."). The rewrite is longer, vaguer, and loses the concrete images. Abstraction here costs both length and information.
- The scrupulous writer's questions: "What am I trying to say? What words will express it? What image or idiom will make it clearer? Is this image fresh enough to have an effect?"
- Six rules (verified verbatim): (i) "Never use a metaphor, simile or other figure of speech which you are used to seeing in print." (ii) "Never use a long word where a short one will do." (iii) "If it is possible to cut a word out, always cut it out." (iv) "Never use the passive where you can use the active." (v) "Never use a foreign phrase, a scientific word or a jargon word if you can think of an everyday English equivalent." (vi) "Break any of these rules sooner than say anything outright barbarous."
- Political application: political language "is designed to make lies sound truthful and murder respectable".

## Relevance to AI answer style
Orwell supplies the vocabulary for the main LLM failure modes:
- "Operators/verbal false limbs" are nominalized padding: "provide assistance with", "make a determination", "it is important to note".
- "Meaningless words" are unfalsifiable praise or hedges: "robust", "comprehensive", "seamless", "leverage".
- "Ready-made phrases" are the stock openers and closers: "Great question!", "I hope this helps".

His diagnosis also bears on PRECISION vs COMPRESSION. The fault is not word count but words chosen before meaning. His Ecclesiastes parody shows abstraction is lossy and longer at the same time. Rule (vi) is an explicit escape hatch: when cutting harms clarity, the rules yield. A harness should keep this priority. Accuracy and clarity outrank brevity, so the rules must not push an answer into telegraphic or ambiguous compression. "Think first, then choose words" ("What am I trying to say?") corresponds to drafting the claim before phrasing it.

## Candidate rules for the harness
1. **Meaning first, then words.** Before answering, state the claim internally in one sentence ("What am I trying to say?"). Every sentence in the reply must serve that claim. Never let a stock phrase pick the content.
2. **Ban false limbs and empty words.** Replace verb-plus-noun operators with the verb ("make a decision" -> "decide", "provide support for" -> "support"). Drop evaluative words that point to nothing checkable ("robust", "seamless", "powerful") unless followed by the concrete property.
3. **Clarity overrides brevity (Orwell's rule vi).** Cut a word only if the meaning survives intact. Never drop articles, connectives, units, conditions or caveats to save words if the result is ambiguous or "barbarous".
