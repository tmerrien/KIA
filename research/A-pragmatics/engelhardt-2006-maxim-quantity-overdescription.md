# Engelhardt, Bailey & Ferreira (2006): Do speakers and listeners observe the Gricean Maxim of Quantity?

## Citation
Engelhardt, P. E., Bailey, K. G. D., & Ferreira, F. (2006). Do speakers and listeners observe the Gricean Maxim of Quantity? *Journal of Memory and Language*, 54(4), 554-573. doi:10.1016/j.jml.2005.12.009

Full text read from the Ferreira lab author copy: https://ferreiralab.faculty.ucdavis.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/222/2015/05/Engelhardt-et-al.-2006_GriceanMaximQuantity_JML.pdf

## Core claim
Speakers almost never under-describe a referent, but they over-describe it on about 30% of trials. In explicit ratings, listeners judge over-descriptions to be no worse than concise descriptions. Eye-tracking shows that the redundant modifier still causes brief confusion that listeners do not notice. The authors conclude that people are "only moderately Gricean": the two halves of the Maxim of Quantity are not equally strong.

## Key evidence
- **Exp. 1, production (n=10, 24 critical displays).** Participants instructed a confederate to move real objects, for example an apple on a towel.
  - Two apples present: the speaker modified the target on 98% of trials, so there was almost no under-description.
  - One apple present: the speaker still modified it ("the apple on the towel") on 30% of trials, which is significantly above 0. The rate was 40% when the destination matched the current location type and 20% when it differed (difference not significant).
  - Destination of the same type as the current location (towel to towel): the speaker marked it with *other*, *opposite* or similar on 96.5% of trials, against 4% when the types differed. Speakers marked whatever was *pragmatically relevant* to them, here the fact that this was a move between two towels.
- **Exp. 2, ratings on a 1-5 scale (n=22).**
  - One-referent display: the under-specified location ("put the apple on the towel" for a towel-to-towel move) was rated worst, at 3.12. Its fix scored 4.74.
  - Two-referent display: a bare target, which is an under-description, scored 2.8 against 4.35 for the modified target.
  - Exp. 2b removed the location confound. Over-described instructions (4.41/4.21) were rated slightly lower than concise ones (4.69/4.64), and the difference was not significant. Listeners do not *explicitly* penalize redundancy.
- **Exp. 3, Visual World eye-tracking (n=20).** Over-described instruction: "put the apple on the towel in the box".
  - Listeners fixated the empty towel right after "on the towel", a garden path.
  - They kept looking at the apple longer.
  - Their looks to the correct goal (the box) were delayed by about 300 ms and were still lower 1800 ms after the utterance ended.
  - Task accuracy was near perfect. The cost was real but brief, and participants were unaware of it.
- **Authors' explanation of over-description.** It reflects the *speaker's* view. The apple-on-towel is a salient compound object, and producing a concise form takes two extra operations: (1) noticing that the listener does not need the modifier, and (2) deleting it. Speakers are imperfect at modeling the listener (they cite Keysar, Barr & Horton 1998; Ferreira et al. 2005). Listeners tolerate over-descriptions because they hear them often. In ratings they may also use a heuristic that "more explicit = better".

## Relevance to AI answer style
- This paper is the empirical boundary between **precision** and **redundancy**.
  - The required marker ("the *other* towel") carries the whole distinction. Leaving it out was the worst-rated error and caused measurable confusion: that is compression failure.
  - The unrequired modifier ("the apple *on the towel*") cost listeners about 300 ms of misdirected processing that they never noticed and did not penalize when rating. Redundancy therefore has a cost that preference ratings do not show.
- For an assistant:
  - Users' stated tastes, such as thumbs-up ratings, will not penalize padding. Comprehension costs still accrue. Thumbs-up feedback is therefore a weak signal for detecting padding.
  - Redundant qualifiers are not neutral. A listener treats a modifier as a cue to a contrast ("which apple?") and goes looking for the alternative. In answers, this means hedges, caveats, or qualifiers that imply a distinction which does not exist ("the *main* issue" when there is only one) can send the reader looking for alternatives that are not there.
  - Over-description comes from the speaker's own view: what was salient to the writer gets mentioned. An assistant that describes things as they appear in its own working state, such as file names, intermediate steps, or what it tried, is making the speaker-perspective error. The fix is the two-step operation the authors name: check whether the reader needs it, then delete it.
  - The rule is asymmetric. Under-description is clearly worse than over-description, both for listeners and in speakers' own behavior. When in doubt, keep the distinguishing term and cut the non-distinguishing one.
- Caveat from the forward literature:
  - Davies & Katsos (2013) show that listeners *do* penalize over-informativeness when the design does not favour it.
  - Rubio-Fernández (2016) and Degen et al. (2020) show that some redundancy speeds identification, when the extra feature is highly discriminable.
  - So redundancy is not always waste. A modifier helps when it makes the referent faster to find and hurts when it implies a contrast that does not exist.

## Candidate rules for the harness
1. **Keep every word that marks a real distinction; cut every word that implies a false one.** When two things of the same kind are in play (two files, two functions, two options), name the distinguishing term ("the *other* config", "the *staging* database"). When only one exists, drop the qualifier: a qualifier makes the reader look for an alternative.
2. **Never under-specify to save words.** Missing a necessary distinguisher was the costliest error in all three experiments. Brevity is not a reason to drop the term that tells the reader *which one*.
3. **Remove details that come only from your own view.** Before sending, check each detail: "Does the reader need this to identify or act on the thing, or was it just salient to me while working?" Cut it if it fails, even when the reader would not object to it. Readers do not notice the cost of padding, but they still pay it.
