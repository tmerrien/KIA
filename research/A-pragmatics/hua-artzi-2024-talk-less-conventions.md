Code (MIT licence): https://github.com/lil-lab/ICCA

Full text read: arXiv PDF v1 (21 pp. including appendices A-D).

## Core claim
Humans in repeated reference games shorten their descriptions over time and converge on stable, partner-specific conventions. Five 2024 multimodal LLMs (IDEFICS-80b-instruct, LLaVa-1.5-13b, GPT-4-vision, Gemini 1.0 Pro Vision, Claude 3 Opus) do not do this spontaneously as speakers, although as listeners they can partly follow a human's increasingly terse language. The strongest models shorten only under heavy, task-specific prompting. Even then they keep introducing new words ("lexical efficiency is not communication efficiency"), so they need a separate explicit consistency instruction before they converge.

## Key evidence
- **Setup.** The data are 54 human-human interactions from Hawkins et al. (2020b). Each game has 4 similar photos and 6 repetitions, for 24 trials. A GPT-4 listener (in the "images once" setting, where it is near human accuracy) evaluates the model speakers. Metrics are message length in IDEFICS tokens, listener accuracy, and a new metric, **Word Novelty Rate (WNR)**: word error rate counting only insertions and substitutions, ignoring deletions, over content words. Dropping words is how conventions form, while adding or substituting words signals new information. WNR tracks the GloVe-similarity curve of Hawkins et al. (2020a) but is sensitive to synonym swaps. The paper's example: once "the car" is established, "the automobile" violates convention stability (App. A.2).
- **S1, plain game instruction.** No model behaves like a human. GPT-4, Gemini and Claude produce *longer* messages in later repetitions and keep a high WNR. This happens at temperature 0, so the novelty is not sampling noise. IDEFICS and LLaVa just repeat their first message and ignore feedback (low accuracy).
- **S2, Gricean quantity instruction** ("as informative as needed, not more", plus "think about how the amount of information needed may change"). GPT-4 and Claude shorten somewhat, but WNR stays far above human levels.
- **S3, explicit "make messages shorter and shorter".** GPT-4 and Gemini shorten but stay longer than humans. Claude reaches human length from a much longer start. All of them substitute new words while shortening, and even when the message cannot get any shorter. Fig. 8 shows the contrast. The human messages run "dirty truck going towards the bridge" -> "dirty truck going to bridge" -> "dirty truck" x3. GPT-4's run "construction bridge with a concrete mixer truck underneath" -> "bridge construction with a white truck" -> "bridge work, dirty truck below" -> "bridge construction" -> "overpass work" -> "concrete mixer".
- **S4, S3 plus a consistency request** ("extract salient tokens from previous messages rather than introducing new words ... keep using that message once it cannot be shortened"). Only this prompt gets GPT-4, Gemini and Claude to match human curves for both length and convergence. The authors note that it is specific to reference games and does not generalise. They conclude that "prompt engineering is not likely to be the solution" (p. 7).
- **Listener side.** With no history (L2), every model gets slightly *worse* as human messages conventionalise (GPT-4 87.5% -> 83.8%). The terse conventions depend on history to be understood. With history and images shown once (L3), all models improve, and GPT-4 and Claude reach 100% against 99.54% for humans. Appendix D (masked and misleading images) shows that much of this gain can come from label-message association in the text history.
- **Repetition bias.** IDEFICS and LLaVa give higher log-probability to a transcript in which the speaker repeats its Repetition-1 messages than to the real, evolving human transcript, in all 54 of 54 interactions (sign test p near 0).
- **Authors' explanation** (p. 10). Unlike humans, models "do not perceive the effort or cost needed for communication, thus having no inherent reason to reduce them."

## Relevance to AI answer style
- **This is direct evidence for the precision/compression distinction in LLM output.** Models shorten by *re-describing*, swapping in different words and new aspects ("overpass work", "concrete mixer"). Humans shorten by *deleting* words from the established description until a stable core remains ("dirty truck"). The human route keeps the exact term that carries the reference. The model route compresses and drifts at the same time, and each new synonym makes the reader re-resolve the referent. The authors use exactly this argument (p. 9), citing Metzing & Brennan (2003).
- **Telling a model to "be concise" or to "follow Grice" is not enough.** Both S2 and S3 produced shorter text with lexical drift. Only an explicit *reuse-the-same-words* constraint (S4) produced human-like conventions. A harness that wants concision across turns must therefore state lexical consistency as its own rule, separate from any length target.
- **Shortening is licensed by shared history, not by the speaker.** Listeners resolved terse human conventions *better* with history and *worse* without it (L2). A shortened term is safe only where the reader saw the full form. Text that leaves the conversation (a summary, a forwarded message) loses that licence.
- **The default LLM drift runs towards longer later turns.** Under S1, frontier models grew their messages over repetitions, which is the opposite of human adaptation. Without a rule, an assistant will tend to re-explain things already grounded.
- **Caveat.** The task is visual reference games with 2024 models. That the result transfers to text-only assistant answers about technical concepts is an inference, not a finding of the paper.

## Candidate rules for the harness
1. **Shorten by deletion, never by substitution.** When you refer again to something already named in the conversation, you may drop modifiers from its earlier full name ("the retry-with-backoff wrapper" -> "the wrapper"). Do not replace its words with synonyms or with a description of a different aspect. Once a short form exists, repeat it verbatim.
2. **Don't re-expand what's grounded.** After a term, definition or explanation has been given and used without confusion, later turns refer to it by its established short form and do not describe it again. Keep "concise" and "consistent" as two separate checks, because meeting one does not ensure the other.
3. **Shortened forms are local.** A shortened term is licensed only for a reader who saw its full form. In any output meant to stand alone (a summary, a handoff, a commit message, a forwarded text), give the full form on first use.
