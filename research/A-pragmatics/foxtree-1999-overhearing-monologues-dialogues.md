# Fox Tree (1999): Listening in on monologues and dialogues

## Citation
Fox Tree, J. E. (1999). Listening in on monologues and dialogues. *Discourse Processes*, 27(1), 35-53. doi:10.1080/01638539909545049

- Publisher's version: closed access (OpenAlex and Semantic Scholar both say CLOSED).
- **Read in full:** the author's accepted manuscript, posted on her lab site with the note "To cite, please refer to: Fox Tree, J. E. (1999)...": https://foxtree.sites.ucsc.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/205/2014/08/foxtree.1999.ms_.pdf (linked from https://foxtree.sites.ucsc.edu/publications/). Page numbers in the final print version were not checked against it. Semantic Scholar gives pp. 35-54, and the author gives 35-53.

## Core claim
People who only listen in, and cannot give feedback, understood spontaneous instructions better from a two-person dialogue than from a one-person monologue on the same task. Neither condition gave them more words or more repetition. The likely cause is that dialogue holds things a monologue lacks. Discourse markers signal the structure of the talk and when repairs are coming. The addressee also offers a second point of view, so the listener has another chance to match the description to the right shape.

## Key evidence
- **Design.** 16 director-matcher pairs did a tangram ordering task: 12 of 16 abstract figures, in "people" and "animal" sets. The two people were in separate rooms and linked only by audio. Directors spoke either with no feedback (monologue; both people knew there would be none, so there was no deception) or in free conversation (dialogue). Recordings were kept only if the original matcher placed every figure correctly. That left 8 monologues and 8 dialogues, paired by figure set and length. 160 usable overhearers each heard one monologue and one dialogue, with order and figure set counterbalanced.
- **Result.** Overhearers made 1.57 errors after a dialogue and 2.27 after a monologue (subjects analysis t1(159) = 2.82, p < .01; items analysis t2(7) = 2.36, p = .05).
- **Confounds ruled out.**
  - Session length: 11:38 for monologues vs 10:52 for dialogues, n.s.
  - Word count: 1355 for monologues vs 1627 for dialogues, n.s. Counting the director alone in dialogues gives 1062, also n.s.
  - Conceptual repetition: 25 repeated concepts in each condition; 35 vs 42 total repetitions, n.s.
  - Restarts: 19 vs 20.
  - Fillers (um and uh): 39 vs 46, n.s.
  - Speech rate differed significantly (t(14) = -2.81), and the text says dialogue speech was faster. The manuscript gives the figures as "115 versus 153 wpm", listed in an order that does not say which number goes with which condition. Either way, the rate difference cannot explain the dialogue advantage.
- **What did differ.** Pragmatic discourse markers (like, oh, well, I mean, you know): about 6 per monologue vs 23 per dialogue (t(14) = -4.58, p = .001). Across the 8 matched pairs, the gap in marker counts correlated with the gap in overhearer errors (r = .73, p < .05). Only 8 data points, and correlational. The author warns that markers may stand in for something else, such as better-organized talk, and asks for an excision test.
- **Second explanation.** A dialogue gives more than one perspective. In one exchange the director said "turkey" and the matcher said "ostrich". The author calls this "conceptual disparity", not repetition, and says it gives the overhearer a second route to the right shape.
- **What overhearers said.** In debriefing, many called the dialogues "desultory, overly repetitive", full of interruptions and "distracting chatter". Yet about 70% of those who said dialogues were harder actually did as well or better on them. What they preferred did not predict what they understood.
- **Why this test is cleaner than earlier ones.** In Kraut, Lewis & Swezey (1982), speakers were secretly cut off from feedback and did not know it. Here both conditions were honest, so the speaker in the monologue knew there would be no feedback and could plan for that.

## Relevance to AI answer style
- **Handoffs and summaries for a third reader.** When an assistant writes a summary that another agent or person will read later, that reader is an overhearer. Schober & Clark showed overhearers lose out when they only see the grounded result, a label such as "the knight", without how it was agreed. This paper adds that seeing *part of the grounding process*, especially the alternative descriptions that were considered and rejected, helped more than a single clean, planned account. This held even though the clean account was no shorter and the reader *preferred* it.
- **Precision vs compression.** What helped here was a *different* point of view on the same thing ("turkey" and "ostrich"), not more words and not repetition. For an AI answer, the precise move is to give the agreed term *plus* the one alternative reading it rules out. Dropping that contrast to save words is compression that loses meaning for anyone who was not in the original exchange.
- **Discourse markers carry signal.** Words like "oh", "well" and "I mean" are often treated as filler to delete. This paper finds they correlate with understanding, plausibly because they signal repairs and structure. In writing, the equivalents are explicit contrast and correction signals such as "not X but Y", "correction:" and "to be clear". Cutting all of them can hurt a reader who is not part of the exchange. The evidence is correlational and from n = 8 pairs, so treat it as weak.
- **Do not trust reader preference.** Overhearers judged the dialogues worse and understood them better. A style judged only by preference ratings can pick the wrong format.

## Candidate rules for the harness
1. **Keep the rejected alternative in handoffs.** When a handoff or summary uses a term or reference that was settled through clarification, keep the settled term and add, in one clause, the reading it was distinguished from ("X, i.e. the one with the leg, not the ostrich-like one"). Do not include the whole back-and-forth.
2. **Keep the corrections a cold reader needs.** In text written for someone who was not there, keep explicit repair and contrast signals ("correction:", "not A but B", "earlier I said X; it is Y"). Cut chatter and politeness padding instead.
3. **Test comprehension, not preference.** When judging answer style for third-party readers, measure whether a reader who was not in the exchange can act on it correctly, not whether they prefer it. Fox Tree's overhearers preferred monologues but understood dialogues better.
