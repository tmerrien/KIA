# Clark & Wilkes-Gibbs (1986): Referring as a collaborative process

## Citation
Clark, H. H., & Wilkes-Gibbs, D. (1986). Referring as a collaborative process. *Cognition*, 22(1), 1-39. doi:10.1016/0010-0277(86)90010-7. PMID 3709088.
Full text read (scanned PDF with a text layer, author's archive): https://web.stanford.edu/~clark/1980s/Clark,%20H.H.%20_%20Wilkes-Gibbs,%20D.%20_Referring%20as%20a%20collaborative%20process_%201986.pdf

## Core claim
A definite reference is not finished when the speaker says the noun phrase. It is finished when both sides accept it. Speaker and addressee present, repair, expand or replace a noun phrase until they reach a version they both accept, and they try to keep their **joint** effort low (the *principle of least collaborative effort*), not just the speaker's word count. A description that has been accepted becomes common ground. Later references can then be shortened to a single unitary term ("the ice skater") that still carries the whole agreed idea.

## Key evidence (from the full text)
- **Task.** A Tangram matching task (after Krauss & Weinheimer 1964): 8 pairs, 12 abstract figures, 6 trials, director and matcher separated by a screen. The transcripts held 9,792 words for 576 placements. The error rate was 2%.
- **Shortening.** Directors used 41 words per figure on trial 1 and 8 words on trial 6 (F(1,35) = 44.31, p < .001). Director turns per figure fell from 3.7 to about 1 (F(1,35) = 79.59). The steepest drop was from trial 1 to trial 2.
- **Canonical exchange.** A "basic exchange" is a short noun phrase followed by "okay". Its share rose over trials 1-6: 18, 55, 75, 80, 88, 84%. Elementary noun phrases rose from 52% to 72%. Provisional and episodic noun phrases declined.
- **Evidence against a plain information-theory account.** Within each trial, words per figure fell from array position 1 to 12. The slope was 4.6 words per position on trial 1, 1.0 on trial 2 and 0.4 on trial 6, and the slopes differed significantly (F(2,284) = 15.49). Shrinking the set of remaining figures cannot explain this, because the set shrinks the same way on every trial. Shared history explains it: once a pair has an accepted label, context matters less.
- **First mention is descriptive, not referential.** On trial 1, directors always *described* figures ("looks like a person who's ice skating...", usually with an indefinite noun phrase). They did not assume a shared perspective existed. From trial 2 on, 89% of initial utterances used a definite identifying reference ("the ice skater").
- **How descriptions shorten.** Descriptions shortened by **simplification**, which drops details: "angel flying away... two arms" became "the angel". They also shortened by **narrowing** to a distinctive part: "the guy in a sleeping bag" became "sleeping bag". Multinary categories became unitary ones. Holistic analogical perspectives beat segmental literal ones: by trial 6, 77% of references were analogical only. Pairs preferred permanent properties such as shape (90% of references) over procedural or temporary ones such as list position (2%).
- **Feedback dropped as common ground grew.** Requests for expansion fell from 36% to 3% of placements. Follow-up checks ("You've got that one, right?") fell from 35% to 5%. Self-repairs fell 85, 30, 20, 8, 7, 6.
- **Criteria.** The *principle of mutual responsibility* says understanding is established "to a criterion sufficient for current purposes", and the criterion goes up as the stakes go up. Listeners face social pressure to accept an elementary presentation, and may even pretend to understand.
- **Writing.** When there is no live feedback, the *principle of distant responsibility* applies: the writer must make sure the reader "should have been able to understand". The authors cite Krauss & Weinheimer (1966): people shorten repeated references less when speaking into a tape recorder, that is, when no partner can confirm.

## Sources it builds on (backward)
- Krauss & Weinheimer (1964, 1966, 1967); Krauss & Glucksberg (1969, 1977). These are the original repeated-reference shortening studies. Shortening happened only when the listener could reply.
- Clark & Marshall (1981), Definite reference and mutual knowledge (common ground as the basis for definite reference).
- Schegloff, Jefferson & Sacks (1977), the preference for self-correction in repair; Sacks, Schegloff & Jefferson (1974), turn-taking; Sacks & Schegloff (1979), try markers.
- Grice (1975), the Quantity maxim ("no more prolix than necessary"). Already covered.
- Zipf (1935), Brown (1958), Olson (1970). These are the least-effort accounts the paper argues against.
- Levelt (1983), self-monitoring and repair; Cohen (1985), spoken versus keyboard instruction.

## Relevance to AI answer style
- **Precision versus compression, empirically.** Shortening "a person who's ice skating, except they're sticking two arms out in front" to "the ice skater" is *precision*: one unitary term carries the whole idea, *because* the longer description was accepted earlier. The same two words on trial 1 would be *compression*, because the listener has no grounded perspective to decode them with. Whether a short term is legitimate depends on the shared history, not on the term itself.
- **First mention must ground.** For a novel or ambiguous concept, describe it the first time in terms the user can check. Coin or adopt a short label only after that. Using a jargon label at first mention is the trial-1 mistake.
- **An assistant's reader is closer to "distant" than "collaborative".** A chat turn gives coarse feedback, like Cohen's keyboard condition. The user often cannot interrupt mid-answer, and silence or continuing does not reliably mean understanding (listeners feign acceptance). The assistant should shorten references less aggressively than a face-to-face partner would, and treat a user's own reuse of a term as the clearest acceptance signal.
- **Least *collaborative* effort is the right objective.** A terse answer that triggers a clarification round costs more in total than a slightly longer one accepted at once. An initial phrase that is too short pushes cost into refashioning.
- **Overhearers and later readers.** Shortened pacts are partner-specific (Schober & Clark 1989; Brennan & Clark 1996). A summary, handoff or document meant for someone else must expand conversation-local labels back to their full form.

## Candidate rules for the harness
1. **Ground before you abbreviate.** At first mention of any non-standard concept, give a short description or definition. Use a compact label (term, acronym, nickname) only after it has appeared with that description in this conversation, or after the user has used it themselves.
2. **Shorten by simplifying, not by truncating.** When reusing an established concept, use the single unitary term that names the whole accepted description ("the ice skater"). Do not use a fragment that drops the distinguishing part, and do not use temporary or procedural handles ("the second option above", "the thing from before") when a permanent, content-based name exists.
3. **Re-expand for a new audience or after a repair.** In any text meant to stand alone (summaries, commit messages, documents for third parties) or right after a misunderstanding, expand conversation-local labels to their full grounded form. These readers are "overhearers", and the label was never grounded with them.
