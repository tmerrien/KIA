## Core claim
Common ground can only be updated through **grounding**: the joint process by which contributor and partner reach the mutual belief that the partner has understood what was meant "to a criterion sufficient for current purposes" (the *grounding criterion*, p. 129). That criterion rises and falls with the **purpose** of the exchange. The **techniques** people use to meet it depend on the **medium**, because each medium changes what each technique costs. People pick whichever available technique minimizes *least collaborative effort*: the work both sides do from the start of a contribution to its mutual acceptance.

## Key evidence and argument (from the full text read)
- **Contribution = presentation + acceptance** (after Clark & Schaefer 1989). Saying an interrogative sentence is not the same as asking a question. The question is asked only once the addressee has given evidence of understanding (the "have a car?" side sequence from the London-Lund corpus). A listener can be in one of four states for any part of an utterance: 0 did not notice, 1 noticed, 2 heard correctly, 3 understood.
- **Positive evidence, not just absence of complaint.** Many models in AI and philosophy treat silence as success (Grosz & Sidner 1986; Litman & Allen 1987; Stalnaker 1978). The authors reject this: "if negative evidence is all we looked for, we would often accept information we had little justification for accepting" (p. 132). There are three kinds of positive evidence: (1) acknowledgments and continuers ("uh huh", "m", nods); (2) **the relevant next turn**, for example an answer that fits the question, which is evidence only if it shows *correct* understanding (Chico: "I live with my brother"); (3) **continued attention**, the most basic. Relevant next turns and continued attention have no separate presentation of their own, which is what stops the infinite regress.
- **Against least effort.** Grice's Quantity and Manner maxims ("be brief") assume flawless presentations and trouble-free acceptances. Under time pressure, error and **ignorance of the addressee**, speakers instead produce *improper* utterances on purpose: try markers ("the person ice skating that has two arms?"), installments, invited completions. These cost the pair less in total than one perfect utterance would. The *principle of least collaborative effort* replaces speaker-only least effort. The preferences for self-repair and self-initiated repair (Schegloff, Jefferson & Sacks 1977) follow from it, because every extra turn adds to the joint cost.
- **Purpose-specific techniques.**
  - *References:* alternative descriptions ("Joe Wright you mean?"), indicative gestures, **referential installments** (secure the referent before predicating anything of it: "Take the spout - the little one that looks like the end of an oil can - / Okay. / and put that on..."), and trial references with a try marker ("a man called Annegra? / Yeah, Allegra"). Left-dislocation looks specialized for this: of the left-dislocated NPs in the London-Lund corpus (Geluykens 1988), 29% were followed by a move from the interlocutor and 52% by a pause where acceptance could happen.
  - *Verbatim content* (numbers, names, addresses): verbatim displays (customers repeated operators' numbers over 70% of the time in Clark & Schaefer 1987), installments sized to memory span (operators always split numbers of 7 or more digits into conventional groups), and spelling or spelling-by-analogy ("Persian like the carpet").
  - The two partners can hold *different* criteria. A father keeps testing a child who thinks he already understands.
- **Eight medium constraints:** copresence, visibility, audibility, cotemporality, simultaneity, sequentiality, reviewability, revisability. Table 1: face-to-face has the first six. Terminal (keyboard) teleconferencing has cotemporality, sequentiality and reviewability. Email and letters have only reviewability and revisability.
- **Eleven grounding costs:** formulation, production, reception, understanding, start-up, delay, asynchrony, speaker change, display, fault, repair. Points that matter here:
  - Losing a constraint does not remove grounding. It changes which technique is cheapest.
  - **Delay costs trade off against formulation costs.** In letters and email, where delay is nearly free, people plan and revise. In speech they must accept imperfect utterances.
  - **High speaker-change cost means "people try to do more within a turn"** (p. 145).
  - **Fault costs depend on the medium.** In email, faults "are not as easily justified, because the sender has already had a chance to revise them".
  - **In non-cotemporal media, other-initiated repair becomes "very costly indeed"**, so speakers "try hard to avoid relying on others to repair misunderstandings. It is less costly for them to revise what they say before sending it" (p. 145).
  - Understanding costs rise when context is missing. An email reader must reconstruct what the message is responding to.
  - People "are willing to use more words talking than in typewriting", and faster typists use more words.
- The chapter's evidence is illustrative: corpus excerpts and summaries of the authors' and others' studies. It reports no new experiment of its own.

## Relevance to AI answer style
- **A chat assistant works in an email-like medium.** It has reviewability and revisability, and a turn is composed before it is sent. It lacks copresence, simultaneity and mid-turn acknowledgment. The user cannot say "m" or frown halfway through a paragraph. Clark & Brennan predict exactly what such a medium demands: pay formulation cost up front, because other-initiated repair is "very costly indeed", and "do more within a turn", because changing speakers is expensive. This is the empirical case against *compression*. A terse answer that saves twenty words but triggers a clarification round raises the total collaborative effort.
- **But fault cost is also higher, and that cuts against padding.** A revisable medium gives no excuse for faults, and that includes imprecise terms. The cheapest way to ground in such a medium is *precision*: the exact term, attached to a check the reader can apply alone (a definition, an example, an alternative description). Restating the same content several ways does not help.
- **The relevant next turn is the assistant's main acceptance evidence.** If the user's follow-up uses the assistant's term correctly, that term is grounded and can be shortened from then on. If the follow-up shows a wrong reading (the Chico case), the term was not grounded, and the user's silence on it earlier meant nothing. Treating "no complaint" as understanding is the negative-evidence error that the authors name.
- **The criterion scales with purpose.** For verbatim-critical content (commands, file paths, numbers, identifiers, names) the grounding criterion is the highest. The right technique is exact, copyable display in conventional chunks, never paraphrase or approximation. For gist-level content a lower criterion is fine, and brevity wins.
- **Referential installments map onto answer structure.** Identify the object being talked about (which file, which function, which option) before saying anything about it, because "until the referent has been properly identified, the rest of the utterance will be difficult, if not impossible, to understand."
- **Try markers have a written equivalent.** When the assistant is unsure the user means X, a brief explicit check ("assuming you mean the staging config...") is the written form of a trial reference. It costs a few words and avoids a whole wasted answer.

## Candidate rules for the harness
1. **Front-load grounding, because repair is expensive here.** In a turn-based text medium, include in the same turn what a face-to-face listener would ask for: secure the referent first (which file, function, option or version), and state any assumption about the user's meaning as a one-clause trial reference. Do not leave ambiguity for the user to repair in a later turn.
2. **Match the grounding technique to the content type.** For verbatim-critical content (commands, paths, identifiers, numbers, names, error strings), give the exact literal in a copyable code span, chunked the way it is conventionally chunked, and never paraphrase it. For conceptual content, ground with one precise term plus one checkable gloss or example, not repeated restatement.
3. **Count only positive evidence as grounding.** Treat a term or label as established only once the user has used it correctly or acted on it correctly. If they have only failed to object, it is not established. Until then, keep the full term. When the user's next turn shows a wrong reading, repair it explicitly before moving on.
