## Core claim
Every declarative sentence splits into *given* information, which the speaker believes the listener already knows, and *new* information. Speaker and listener follow a tacit contract, which the authors present as one part of Grice's cooperative principle. Under it the listener treats the given part as an address: they look for its unique antecedent in memory and attach the new part there. When no direct antecedent exists, the listener has to do extra inferential work (bridging or addition), and that work takes measurable time. Bad packaging (restructuring, ambiguity) makes a sentence feel awkward or unacceptable.

## Key evidence
- **1974, Experiment I.** Sixteen subjects saw 68 context-target pairs and pressed a button when they understood the target. The target *The beer was warm* took 835 ms after a direct antecedent (*We got some beer out of the trunk*). After a context that needed a bridging inference (*We checked the picnic supplies*), it took 1016 ms. The difference was 181 ms, minF'(1,36) = 6.47, p < .025.
- **1974, Experiment II** tests whether repetition explains the result. Context *Andrew was especially fond of beer* repeats the word *beer* but does not establish that any particular beer exists. Targets after a direct antecedent still took 1031 ms against 1168 ms after this context, a 137 ms difference, minF'(1,23) = 15.7, p < .001. The cost comes from failing to find a referent, not from a missing repeated word.
- **1974, Experiment III** (27 subjects) generalizes the effect to presuppositional adverbs (*still, again, too, either*). Means were 1023 ms with a direct antecedent, 1097 ms indirect and 1088 ms negative (Table 1), minF'(2,118) = 5.55, p < .005. Repeating the word versus using a synonym made no reliable difference (1033 vs 1014 ms).
- **1974, caveat from the authors.** The measure is a subjective feeling of having understood, "so the absolute differences between conditions cannot be taken very seriously". The direction of the effect is reliable. The exact size in milliseconds is not.
- **1977, the formal statement.**
  - *Maxim of Antecedence*: "Try to construct your utterance such that the listener has one and only [one] direct antecedent for any given information and that it is the intended antecedent." The authors treat it as part of Grice's maxim of Manner.
  - *Given-New Contract*: build each utterance so that the listener "is able to compute from memory the unique antecedent that was intended for the given information" and "will not already have the new information attached to the antecedent."
- **1977, Table 2: what can and cannot be violated.**
  - The Maxim of Antecedence may be broken on purpose. The listener then bridges or adds a referent and draws an implicature.
  - **Appropriateness** (given must be known, new must be unknown) may not be broken. A breach forces restructuring, and the listener judges the sentence awkward.
  - **Uniqueness** may not be broken. If two antecedents are possible, the sentence is unacceptable.
  - **Computability** may not be broken. If the listener lacks the knowledge to build the bridge, the sentence is incomprehensible.
- **1977, why given comes before new.** The three steps of the strategy have to run in order: identify the given part, find its antecedent, attach the new part. When new comes first, the listener "must hold the new information in abeyance ... This increases the load on his memory", so "the strategy applies most efficiently when given comes before new." This is a processing argument. The 1974 experiments do not test word order directly. They test whether an antecedent is present.
- **1977, three kinds of violation.** *Negligent*: the speaker misjudges what the listener knows, and comprehension slows or fails. *Covert*: deliberate deception. *Explicit*: flagged on purpose to produce an implicature. Their example of negligent violation is Bransford & Johnson's (1973) laundry paragraph, which is unintelligible without its topic. In other words, failing to name the topic is itself a negligent breach.

## Relevance to AI answer style
- This is the empirical basis for "old before new". Haviland & Clark measured the direction of the effect directly: a sentence whose given part has no antecedent takes longer to understand, and the cost comes from the inference the reader has to make. Their own processing argument and the linguistic data support the word-order corollary (given early in the sentence). The reading-time data do not test it.
- **PRECISION vs COMPRESSION.** The contract shows exactly where compression breaks. A definite reference such as *the fix*, *the config* or *that error* is cheap only when the reader has exactly one antecedent for it. Remove the sentence that introduces the referent and the words that remain become a bridging problem (it costs time), an ambiguity (two candidate antecedents, so the sentence is unacceptable) or an impossible computation (incomprehensible). Precision means picking the term whose antecedent is unique and already in the reader's memory. Compression drops the antecedent and hands the inference to the reader.
- **Computability is judged against the reader, not the writer.** An assistant's working context (files it read, tool output, its private reasoning) is not the user's memory. Pointing at "the function above" or "the issue we saw" when the user never saw it is a negligent violation, the case Clark & Haviland predict will slow or derail comprehension.
- The *new* half of the contract (b) forbids attaching information the reader already holds. That gives a principled reason to cut restatements of the user's question or of facts they just supplied: such content is presented as new but carries nothing new.
- The authors also allow deliberate violations. Introducing a term with a definite article (the "addition" route) works when the reader can tell that a new referent is being set up. A label used once and defined in place is acceptable. A label that is never defined is not.

## Candidate rules for the harness
1. **Introduce before you point.** Before using a definite reference (*the X*, *this*, *that approach*, an acronym or codename), make sure the user saw its antecedent, whether in their message or earlier in the visible answer, and that exactly one candidate fits. Otherwise name it in full on first mention. Do not treat tool output or internal reasoning the user never saw as given.
2. **Given first, new last.** Open each sentence or paragraph with the thing the user already knows (their question's subject, the item just discussed) and put the new claim, number or recommendation after it. Do not open with an unexplained conclusion that the reader has to hold in memory until its anchor arrives.
3. **Never present given information as new.** Do not restate what the user just told you as though it were information. Spend words only on content that is new to them, and keep only the minimal given phrase needed to anchor it.
