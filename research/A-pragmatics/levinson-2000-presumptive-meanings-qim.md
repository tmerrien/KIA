## Core claim
Between sentence meaning and the speaker's meaning in context there is a third level: **utterance-type meaning**. It consists of default, defeasible "presumptive" inferences that "are carried by the structure of utterances, given the structure of the language, and not by virtue of the particular contexts of utterances" (2000: 1, via Carston). Three heuristics generate these generalized conversational implicatures (GCIs):
- **Q:** what isn't said, isn't.
- **I:** what is simply described is stereotypically exemplified.
- **M:** what's said in an abnormal way isn't normal.

When the heuristics conflict, they apply in the order Q > M > I. The reason this system exists is that articulation is the slow bottleneck of communication, so the speaker can say little and leave the hearer to infer much.

## Key evidence
- **Heuristic wordings in the book** (2000: 31-33, via Green 2001):
  - Q: "What isn't said, isn't." Example: "There's a blue pyramid on the red cube" implicates that there is no cone on the cube and no red pyramid.
  - I: "What is simply described is stereotypically exemplified." Example: "The blue pyramid is on the red cube" implicates that the pyramid sits directly on the cube.
  - M: "What's said in an abnormal way, isn't normal; or Marked message indicates marked situation." Example: "The blue cuboid block is supported by the red cube" implicates that the block is "not, strictly, a cube" and that the support is "not directly or centrally or stably" given.
- **Ordering** (Green; Carston p. 183): Q-licensed GCIs win over M-licensed ones, which win over I-licensed ones. The 1995 chapter (p. 106) also says "Q1 and M inferences take precedence over inconsistent Q2 inferences" (Q2 is the I-heuristic).
- **The articulation bottleneck** (1995: 95-96, section titled "Overcoming the bottleneck in human communication: Grice's maxims as heuristics"):
  - Articulation is "a relatively slow and inefficient process, which acts as a bottleneck". The other processes in production and comprehension "could run three to four times faster". Footnote 7 puts the rate of human speech "still under 100 BAUD", assuming 7 syllables or 17.5 segments per second and 5.5 bits per phoneme.
  - Hence "any trade-off from coded content to inferential meaning may greatly increase the speed of communication: it will pay to say little and infer much, provided of course the inferential content can be recovered (a) reliably, and (b) speedily."
  - Nonce inference about intentions is neither reliable nor fast. Default heuristics are, and by combining them "we can greatly amplify the content of what we say" (1995: 98).
  - The line usually quoted, "inference is cheap, articulation expensive", is attributed to the 2000 book by Piantadosi et al. (2012). I did not see it in the pages read, so it is UNVERIFIED in the primary text.
- **The M-heuristic as stated in 1995** (p. 97): "'Marked descriptions warn "marked situation"'. Constraint: only of marked, unusual or periphrastic expressions. Characteristics: metalinguistic (marked compared to unmarked); the inference is to the complement of the inference that would have been induced by the unmarked expression."
  - The relevant sense of markedness "is very broad, covering formal prolixity, infrequent expressions or those of unusual formation" (p. 104).
  - Q2 (I) and M are iconic. "Minimal expressions invite stereotypical, rich interpretations" (p. 103). "Non-stereotypical expressions invite interpretations to non-stereotypical extensions" (p. 104).
- **Minimal pairs** (1995: 104-105). In each, the unmarked form gets the I-reading and the marked form gets the M-reading.
  - "John could solve the problem" implicates "and he did". "John had the ability to solve the problem" implicates "but he didn't".
  - "James stopped the car" implicates the normal way, with the foot pedal. "James caused the car to stop" implicates a non-stereotypical way, such as the hand-brake.
  - "It's possible he will recover" suggests a definite probability p. "It's not impossible that he will recover" suggests some probability lower than p.
  - "The mail is reliable" means reliable to degree n. "The mail is not unreliable" means reliable to a degree lower than n.
  - "He ate and ate" implicates that he ate more than the normal meal.
  - Lexical doublets: *knife* vs *cutter* ("not a normal knife"), *house* vs *residence*, *rare* vs *scarce*.
- **Q-heuristic on scales** (1995: 98-99):
  - Scales: ⟨all, some⟩, ⟨and, or⟩, ⟨since, if⟩, ⟨hot, warm⟩, ⟨necessary, possible⟩.
  - A superordinate term implicates that the speaker does not know the more specific one. "I saw an animal in the larder" implicates "I don't know whether it was a mouse, a rat, a squirrel or what."
  - The speaker "committed himself by a turn of phrase to an interpretation that he knows the recipient will make" (1995: 98).
- **GCIs are generalized, not particularized** (Carston p. 182). "Some of them got stomach flu" carries the GCI "not all" in every context. The PCI "camp didn't go well" arises only in reply to a question about the camp.
- **Pragmatic intrusion** (Carston pp. 183-184; 2000: 213-214). In conditionals, comparatives and negations, a GCI can enter the truth conditions. "If both teams got three goals the game was a draw" is read as "exactly three".
- **Counter-evidence** (Carston pp. 185-186; SEP "Defaults in Semantics and Pragmatics" §2, https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/defaults-semantics-pragmatics/):
  - Bott & Noveck (2003 ms., published 2004) found more literal "true" answers to "Some elephants are mammals" under time pressure: 72% at a 900 ms lag versus 56% at 3 s. That is the reverse of what an automatic-default account predicts.
  - Breheny et al. (2006) also argue against scalar defaults. The *content* of the inferences is not disputed. Their *automaticity* is.

## Relevance to AI answer style
1. **The bottleneck has moved, but the logic still holds.** For an LLM, producing text is cheap. The scarce channel is the reader's reading time: skimming runs at roughly 200-300 wpm (UNVERIFIED here, see track F). Levinson's "say little and infer much, provided the inference is (a) reliable and (b) speedy" carries over directly. The assistant may omit anything the reader will infer *by default*: the stereotypical case (I) and the negation of stronger alternatives (Q). It must not omit anything that needs *nonce*, context-specific reasoning to recover, because that kind of inference is "neither speedy nor reliable". This gives an operational line between PRECISION and COMPRESSION:
   - **Precision** leaves out only what Q/I defaults restore.
   - **Compression** leaves out what only a bespoke inference could restore.
2. **The M-heuristic makes verbosity carry meaning.**
   - A periphrastic or hedged phrase is read as "the abnormal case". "Not unreliable" is read as *less* reliable than "reliable". "Was able to fix it" is read as *didn't*. "Caused the test to pass" is read as a hack.
   - Habitual hedging ("it may be possible that...", "it is not impossible") and inflated synonyms ("utilize" for "use") therefore *signal* doubt or a non-standard situation the writer did not mean. Padding is not a neutral cost: the reader will draw a wrong inference from it.
3. **Q works on lexical scales.** A weaker term or a superordinate term implicates ignorance or denial of the stronger one. "Some tests fail" implies not all. "Saw an animal" implies the speaker doesn't know which. "A config file" implies the speaker doesn't know which one.
   - The precise term (the hyponym, the strongest true scalar term) is therefore not just shorter. It also blocks a false "speaker doesn't know" implicature.
   - Because Q outranks M and I, a weak or general term overrides any stereotype the reader would otherwise assume.
4. **Defaults are cancellable, but cancelling costs words.** An answer that relies on a default must check that the default holds. If it doesn't, the answer must state the exception explicitly. In Levinson's account, defaults are cancelled when they are inconsistent with what is already taken for granted.

## Candidate rules for the harness
1. **Plain form for the plain case; marked form only for a marked case (I/M).** Use the shortest standard term ("reliable", "fixed", "use", "stopped") when the normal situation holds. Use a double negative, a periphrastic causative, a modal of ability or an inflated synonym only when you *mean* the non-standard reading. In that case, also say how it differs. Never say "not unreasonable" or "was able to" as a stylistic softener.
2. **Strongest true term, most specific known term (Q).** Pick the strongest scalar item the evidence supports (all, always, must, fixed). Pick the most specific name you know (the file path, the function, the version). A weaker or vaguer word will be read as "not the stronger one" or "I don't know which".
3. **Omit only what a default restores (the bottleneck criterion).** Drop words when the reader will infer the content by Q or I default: the stereotypical case, temporal order in "A and B", the normal method. Keep words whose content the reader could recover only by reasoning about this particular context. That is compression, and it is unreliable.
