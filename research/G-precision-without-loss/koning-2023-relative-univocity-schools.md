## Core claim (max 3 sentences)

Every school of terminology after Wüster rejects his *Eineindeutigkeitspostulat* (EEP, the one-term-one-concept postulate), but even the descriptive schools cannot do without some prescriptive unambiguity. This applies to Roelcke's pragmalinguistic and cognitive models and to Temmerman's sociocognitive approach. Wüster already supplied the workable middle position: **relative Eineindeutigkeit**, meaning unambiguity in context. Köning argues that it is the better alternative, both to the absolute EEP and to pure description.

## Key evidence (page numbers are trans-kom pages; quotations are verbatim from the article)

- **The postulate** (p. 96, quoting Wüster 1931/1966: 94): "Möglichst wenige Ausdrücke sollen mehrdeutig (Homonymie) oder mit anderen gleichbedeutend (Synonymie) sein." Köning adds:
  - Wüster borrowed *Eineindeutigkeit* from mathematics and logic, where it means a one-to-one mapping (citing Gerzymisch-Arbogast 1996: 10).
  - The aim follows the general principle of linguistic economy.
- **Relative univocity** (p. 97, quoting Wüster 1966: 95, via Gerzymisch-Arbogast 1996: 3): "Es genügt, wenn die Bezeichnungen im Zusammenhang eindeutig sind: relative Eineindeutigkeit." Köning's point is that much of the reception overlooks this, so critics attack a straw-man Wüster.
- **Pragmalinguistic critique, Roelcke 1991** (pp. 97-100):
  - Polysemy and synonymy "nicht als Schwächen, sondern … in den wissenschaftlichen und technischen Kommunikationsbedürfnissen selbst begründete Charakteristika" (Roelcke 1991: 206).
  - Evidence 1: the polysemy of *Vernunft* in Kant.
  - Evidence 2: different names for the same parts in different Bosch departments.
  - Roelcke still allows synonymy only as long as the exact use of each term is made clear in context, and polysemy only when each sense is marked (p. 99).
  - Köning finds Roelcke's "kommunikative Eindeutigkeit" hard to tell apart from Wüster's relative univocity. He also notes that in practice, technical texts usually do *not* mark synonyms (p. 100).
- **Cognitive model, Roelcke 2004/2016/2018** (pp. 100-104):
  - Too much precision is "effektiv" but not "effizient" in terms of language economy (Roelcke 2018: 501, as summarised by Köning).
  - Synonyms can bring out particular aspects of a meaning; "der kommunikative Aufwand und das kommunikative Ergebnis [haben] in einem optimalen Verhältnis zueinander zu stehen" (Roelcke 2016: 98).
  - Ambiguity is manageable if the texts "tatsächlich über entsprechende Kontextindikatoren verfügen" (Roelcke 2016: 97).
  - Köning's verdict: Roelcke reuses Wüster's notion of "relative" unambiguity without crediting it.
- **Same aspect, different context** (p. 103, quoting Wüster 1966: 96):
  - Synonyms are unavoidable "wenn sie eine verschiedene Auffassung desselben Begriffs zum Ausdruck bringen". *Kochsalz* names the substance by use and appearance, *Natriumchlorid* by composition.
  - Köning's gloss: *Natriumchlorid* in a kitchen would confuse, and *Kochsalz* in a chemistry text would signal a lack of expertise. So the right term is relative to the audience, not absolute.
  - Wüster also: synonyms "beeinträchtigen die Bequemlichkeit, weil sie das Gedächtnis unnötig … beschweren" (1931/1966: 96).
- **Sociocognitive critique, Temmerman** (pp. 104-107):
  - Temmerman 2000: 17: "The univocity [...] principle is untenable, but artificially maintained. Like polysemy, synonymy is also a fact whose function is apparent."
  - Temmerman 2000: 15: traditional terminology "confuses principles … with facts."
  - Felber (1984: 183-186, via Temmerman 2000: 10) splits univocity into **monosemy** (one concept per term) and **mononymy** (one term per concept).
  - Temmerman 2017: 88 concedes: "In case of a clear-cut concept the univocity principle of traditional Terminology may be applied in terminography." Fuzzier, migrating terms such as *cloning* resist it.
  - Socioterminology (Freixa 2016: 329): biunivocity "no es más que un ideal."
- **Translation of the term itself** (p. 106): rendering *Eineindeutigkeit* as English *univocity* is itself ambiguous, because it suggests mere unambiguity rather than a two-way 1:1 mapping. Köning suggests *biunivocity* is less ambiguous. This is a small worked example of the precision problem.
- **Conclusion, the didactic argument** (p. 108):
  - Introductory texts in particular "benötigen … eine gewisse terminologische Konsistenz, um aus Laien Experten zu machen."
  - Basic terms (for example *Morphem* = smallest meaning-bearing unit) must hold regardless of context. Otherwise *Morphem* and *Silbe* could be used as synonyms just because a learner thinks of them that way.

## Relevance to AI answer style

The paper settles the question the Wüster note left open: **how strict should "one concept, one term" be?** The answer is **relative univocity**. A term has to be unambiguous *within this answer, for this reader*, not across all discourse.

The descriptive critics (Roelcke, Temmerman) argue that synonyms and polysemy carry real information, such as aspect, register and discipline. Even they accept this only when the text supplies **context indicators** that mark which sense or which aspect is meant. Köning notes that real technical texts usually fail to supply them.

For an assistant this gives three points:

1. **Precision is audience-relative.** *Kochsalz* vs. *Natriumchlorid*: the exact term is the one that, for this reader, names the concept from the relevant aspect. A more technical word is not automatically more precise. Used for the wrong audience it is a register error.
2. **Variation needs a marker.** Unmarked synonym variation (the "Bosch departments" problem) costs memory, in Wüster's words, and signals a second concept. Marked variation can add information, in Roelcke's terms.
3. **Consistency matters most when teaching.** Explanations to newcomers need the strictest term consistency, because the reader cannot yet reconstruct the intended concept from context.

The Roelcke point that "too much precision is effective but not efficient" is the compression side of the trade-off. It argues against definitional over-specification where context already disambiguates.

## Candidate rules for the harness

1. **Relative univocity check.** Before finalising, check that each key term has exactly one sense *within the answer*. If a word is used in two senses (for example "model" as ML model and as data model), rename one of them or mark it explicitly at the switch.
2. **Mark every deliberate synonym.** Use a second name for a concept only to bring out a different aspect or to match the reader's register. Signal it on first use ("sodium chloride, i.e. table salt"), then stick to the one the reader will keep using. Never vary terms for style.
3. **Scale term strictness to reader expertise.** For a novice reader, define the core term once by its distinguishing feature and hold it fixed. For an expert reader in a context that already disambiguates, skip the definition: over-specifying is precision that is effective but not efficient.
