## Core claim

Clause 4.1.1 sets the basic principle of terminology work: "one designation (i.e. term, symbol or appellation) corresponds to one concept and only one concept corresponds to one designation in a given domain or subject in a given language". Standardization must therefore avoid one term covering several concepts and several terms covering one concept. It must define only the concepts relevant to the scope, and every definition must take a form that "can replace the term in context (i.e. principle of substitution)". Once a term is fixed, it must be "used consistently" throughout the document (4.2).

## Key evidence (verbatim from the 2011 preview unless marked)

- **4.1.1**: "a) every effort shall be made to avoid use of a single term for multiple concepts and multiple terms for a single concept, b) every effort shall be made to avoid contradictions occurring in terminological entries in closely related standards, c) only the concepts relevant to the domain, subject or scope of the standard shall be defined, and d) the form of a definition shall be such that it can replace the term in context (i.e. principle of substitution)."
- **4.1.1, concession**: "it is acknowledged that there are many cases across different domains or subjects where several terms correspond to a given concept and where several concepts are represented by the same term (i.e. a homograph)." Univocity is a target, not a description of language.
- **4.2**: "The terminological entries shall cover all concepts relevant to the domain, subject and scope, minimizing overlap with other standards. Within any standard, the terms specified shall be used consistently."
- **3.4.2.1 definition**: "representation of a concept by a descriptive statement which serves to differentiate it from related concepts" [from ISO 1087-1:2000, 3.3.1]. In this standard, a definition's job is *differentiation from neighbours*, not exhaustive description.
- **3.4.1.4 homograph, EXAMPLE 1**: "The homographic term 'die' as a noun represents different concepts in the domains of manufacturing, integrated circuits and table-top games." A term is precise only relative to a domain.
- **3.4.1.2.4 abbreviated form**: "representation of a designation resulting from the omission of any part of the full form" ("CD" for "compact disc"). The standard treats an abbreviation as another designation of the *same* concept. Shortening the form is legitimate when the concept is unchanged.
- **Clause 3 preamble, a worked case of lossless simplification**: entries "have been modified to meet the needs of the target group". Notes on 3.4.1.3.2 and 3.4.1.3.3 say the ISO 1087-1 definition "was not considered easily understandable by the target group ... Therefore, the definition has been rewritten without changing the substance of the original definition." 3.4.1.3.1 drops "according to the scale of the term acceptability rating" to make the definition "more easily understandable ... without changing the substance". The standard separates rewording for the audience from changing the content, and it logs each modification ("modified —").
- **3.1.1 source note, removing a hedge**: "By omitting 'usually' in front of 'accompanied by their definitions' this terminological entry is made consistent with the other terms and definitions". Hedges count as content and are edited deliberately, not dropped for brevity.
- **4.1.2 b)** (multilingual): definitions in different languages "shall be equivalent in content and, when possible, similar structures shall be used in their formulation." **4.1.2 d)**: usage-specific information "shall be added as a secondary data category ... or shall be placed in a note to entry". Non-defining material is moved out of the definition.
- **Definition form, probably the 1992 edition (secondary source, HTA Glossary; UNVERIFIED for 2011)**: "The definition shall have the same grammatical form as the term." "The preferred structure of a definition is: a basic part stating the class to which the concept belongs" [plus distinguishing characteristics]. "Unless there is a specific reason, the definition shall not begin with an article." "The definition shall consist of a single phrase describing the concept denoted by the term."

## Relevance to AI answer style

This standard provides three rules that 704 only implies. (1) **Univocity within a document**: once an answer uses a term for a concept, it should keep using that term. Swapping in a synonym for stylistic variety ("elegant variation") is a hidden COMPRESSION failure, because the reader cannot tell whether the new word names a new concept. (2) **Define only what is in scope** (4.1.1 c): precision does not require defining everything, only the concepts the answer depends on. (3) **Lossless rewording is a separate, auditable act** (Clause 3 "modified —" notes): the standard rewrites definitions for its target audience, explicitly "without changing the substance". It also treats removing "usually" as a change of content that must be justified. This is the PRECISION vs COMPRESSION line drawn by a standards body: simplify the form for the reader, never the claim, and treat hedges and qualifiers as content.

## Candidate rules for the harness

1. **One term per concept per answer.** Once a concept is named, reuse the same term every time. Do not alternate synonyms for variety, and never use one term for two different things in the same answer. If the user's word differs from the technical term, map it once ("X, which you called Y") and then stick to one.
2. **Simplify wording, not substance; hedges are substance.** When rephrasing for a less expert reader, keep every truth-conditional qualifier ("usually", "in v2+", "on Linux"). Removing one is a change to the claim, so do it only if the claim really holds without it.
3. **Define only in-scope, ambiguous terms, in one substitutable phrase.** Gloss a term only if the answer depends on it and it is ambiguous across domains (like "die"). Use one phrase of the same grammatical form as the term, starting from its class, so that it could replace the term in the sentence.
