# Jiang, Liu & Van Durme (2025): Conformal Linguistic Calibration

## Citation
Jiang, Z., Liu, A., & Van Durme, B. (2025). *Conformal Linguistic Calibration: Trading-off between Factuality and Specificity.* arXiv:2502.19110 (v1 26 Feb 2025; v4 11 Aug 2025; the v4 PDF says "Preprint. Under review"). Johns Hopkins University.

## Core claim
Abstention ("I don't know") and hedging ("Possibly Los Angeles") are two ends of one operation: choosing a set of possible worlds the speaker is committed to. Instead of hedging a specific claim, a model should rewrite it into a *less specific but unhedged* claim (e.g. "a coastal city") that covers every answer it still finds plausible and excludes the ones it rules out. Calibrated with Learn-Then-Test, this gives a distribution-free guarantee on factual accuracy and a smooth trade-off between factuality and informativeness.

## Key evidence
- **Formalism (Sec. 2-3).** Kripke/Hintikka possible-world semantics, plus Goodman's (2024) nested "spheres" view of degrees of confidence. A claim c̃ *describes* a sphere V iff it is necessarily true across V and not possibly true outside V: (□_V c̃) ∧ ¬◇_{W\V} c̃ (Def. 3.1). Theorem 3.2: if b1 entails b2, then V1 ⊆ V2. So weaker claims correspond to wider belief sets.
- **Pipeline.** Sample K=100 answers from Llama-3-8B-Instruct. Have an LLM name the answer clusters and count each cluster's multiplicity. Seed the set with the majority answer, then grow nested sets by *semantic proximity*, not frequency: for "When did Brexit happen?", add 2019 before 2016 even when 2016 is more frequent. The reason is that frequency-ordered sets are hard to summarise in one claim. GPT-4o then writes, for each set, a claim that covers the "could be correct" answers and excludes the "definitely wrong" ones. The prompt frames the clusters as an error-prone respondent's beliefs; the authors call this "crucial", because without it the LLM ignores the wrong clusters. In a manual check by one author, 11 items per answer type, the summaries scored precision 0.81 and recall 0.91.
- **Guarantee (Sec. 4.1, Fig. 3).** On SimpleQA and NQ, test-set factuality tracks the lower bound of the conformal guarantee closely, so the method does not drift into trivially true answers. At matched coverage it beats abstention by a large margin on SimpleQA, and unlike abstention it answers every question. With enough generalisation, the 7B/8B model passes GPT-4o's SimpleQA factuality. Example ladder: "Kiev/Kyiv" (wrong), then "a major cultural and historical center in central or eastern Ukraine" (wrong), then "a major cultural and historical center of Ukraine" (correct).
- **Cost in information (Sec. 4.2, Fig. 4).** Informativeness, measured as CPMI (conditional pointwise mutual information), falls smoothly as factuality rises. A naive "rewrite to be more confident" prompt gives no fine-grained control: it "easily degenerates to rewrites with low informativeness."
- **Amortised rewriter (Sec. 4.3).** SFT, DPO and ORPO rewriters were trained on 3,770 rewrite instances. On Core-filtered FActScore biographies, all three raise FActScore as the requested confidence goes up (guess → somewhat confident → confident → certain), and they follow the requested level better than the base model.
- **Evaluation design (App. B, Table 4).** The grader prompt treats "Living thing" as CONSISTENT with the gold answer "Lion", and "Somebody wrote it" as consistent with Fitzgerald: "A response being too general is not a reason to mark it as INCONSISTENT." A hedged answer still counts as wrong if it contradicts the gold answer. So factuality here *by construction* rewards vagueness, and only the CPMI curve measures what that vagueness costs.
- **Failure cases in their own outputs (App. D, Table 6).** These are worth knowing. "The number of ODIs played by Carl Rackemann is a positive integer." "Tadashi Imai died sometime between the 1960s and 2000." Some answers collapse to "the date ... is uncertain or not specifically known." At the "Conf." level, "Lost in White is a horror-thriller" turns into a longer paraphrase with the same content. Several rewrites are both **longer and less informative** than the source.
- **"Pragmatics" claim.** The abstract says the framework connects abstention and linguistic calibration "through the lens of linguistic pragmatics". But the body (v4, and v1 checked by grep) does not cite Grice, relevance theory or any implicature work. The machinery is modal *semantics* (Kripke, Hintikka, Lewis 1973 spheres, Stalnaker 1984, Goodman 2024, Cariani et al. 2018). The pragmatic reading (a vaguer claim implicates the speaker cannot say more, via Quantity) is left implicit.

## Relevance to AI answer style (precision vs compression)
- CLC gives an operational definition of **false precision**: a claim whose sphere is narrower than the speaker's belief set. "Los Angeles", said by a model that splits its belief between NYC, LA and Chicago, is falsely precise. The honest fix is not "Possibly Los Angeles", which keeps the false specificity and adds a vague modal. The fix is the most specific claim that is still *necessary* across the beliefs the speaker retains. That is precision measured against what the speaker knows, not against the world.
- It also shows that **generalisation is not free compression**. CPMI drops monotonically, so each step up the ladder throws away information on purpose. A rewrite counts as good only if it stops at the *tightest* true level. Their own failures ("a positive integer", "is uncertain") show where generalisation becomes vacuity. A vacuous claim is short, unfalsifiable, and worthless, the same failure as over-compression.
- A practical point for phrasing: an unhedged coarse claim (a coastal city; between 1960 and 2000; a district of Maharashtra) is usually easier for the reader to use than a hedged fine claim. Hedges are hard to interpret and break downstream use (they cite Lee et al. 2024). When a hedge is used, it should mark a real belief boundary.
- Caveat for a style harness: the paper's factuality metric counts any generalisation as correct. "More factual" in this paper is therefore cheap, and a harness that rewarded only correctness would push answers towards vagueness. Score specificity and correctness together, as Wei et al.'s F1@K tries to do, and as this paper's CPMI curve shows is necessary.

## Candidate rules for the harness
1. **Commit at the tightest level you can defend.** When unsure between specific alternatives, state the most specific claim that covers *all* of them and none of the rejected ones ("a West Coast port", "between 2019 and 2020"). Do not pick one alternative and add "possibly". Name the alternatives when they are few.
2. **A generalisation must exclude something.** Before sending a backed-off claim, check that it rules out a plausible wrong answer. If it does not ("is a positive integer", "is not precisely known"), it is vacuous. Say plainly what is unknown, or abstain on that claim, instead of padding.
3. **Hedge words mark belief boundaries, not tone.** Use a confidence marker only when it changes how the reader should act. Never use one to soften a claim you could state more coarsely and with certainty. Do not stack a hedge on top of an already-generalised claim.
