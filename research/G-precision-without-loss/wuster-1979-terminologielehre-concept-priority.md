## Core claim (max 3 sentences)

Terminology starts from the **concept**, not the word. Concepts are fixed by their characteristics, placed in a **concept system**, and then given the designation that best fits the definition. The ideal mapping between term and concept is **one-to-one (Eineindeutigkeit, univocity)**: no homonyms or polysemes, and no synonyms. Wüster himself treats this ideal as relative to context and gives way where a synonym carries a different conceptual view. Language is judged as a means of transport for thought: "convenience" (low effort) is weighed against "precision" (low "loss of cargo" between the idea the speaker intends and the idea the hearer actually forms).

## Key evidence (quotations as reproduced in the sources)

- **Concept systems come first** (1979, p. 1, Savatovsky's English): "the primacy of conceptual systems brings general [terminology] closer to contextual linguistics and structural linguistics … Because of the pre-eminence of conceptual research, this discipline must collaborate with other formal sciences."
- **Univocity** (1979, p. 79, via Savatovsky): in terminology "there should not be [...] names that have several meanings (homonyms and polysemes), nor should there be several names for a single concept (synonyms)."
- **Univocity relaxed** (1979, p. 98, via Savatovsky): "in terminology itself, synonyms with differences in their Sinnform cannot be completely rejected." Savatovsky concludes that biunivocity is "more a regulative idea than a constitutive principle" of the theory.
- **Same idea in the earlier work** (Wüster 1931/1966, pp. 94-96, via Köning 2023):
  - The basic requirement for linking form to meaning is Eineindeutigkeit: "Möglichst wenige Ausdrücke sollen mehrdeutig (Homonymie) oder mit anderen gleichbedeutend (Synonymie) sein" (as few expressions as possible should be ambiguous or synonymous with others).
  - "Im allgemeinen ist absolute Eineindeutigkeit […] gar nicht erforderlich. Es genügt, wenn die Bezeichnungen im Zusammenhang eindeutig sind: relative Eineindeutigkeit" (absolute univocity is generally not needed; it is enough for designations to be unambiguous in context). Köning quotes this through Gerzymisch-Arbogast 1996.
  - Synonyms "beeinträchtigen die Bequemlichkeit, weil sie das Gedächtnis unnötig … beschweren" (they impair convenience because they burden memory unnecessarily).
  - Synonyms are unavoidable "wenn sie eine verschiedene Auffassung desselben Begriffs zum Ausdruck bringen": *Kochsalz* names the concept by use and appearance, *Natriumchlorid* by composition.
- **Precision against effort** (Wüster 1931/1970, p. 85, via Budin 2022): "Bei der Sprache nennt man geringen Energieverbrauch Bequemlichkeit und geringen 'Ladungsverlust' Genauigkeit; ein Verständigungsmittel ist umso genauer, je mehr die im Partner wirklich hervorgerufene Vorstellungsreihe mit der beabsichtigten Vorstellungsreihe übereinstimmt." In English: in language, low energy use is called convenience and low "loss of cargo" is called precision; a means of communication is more precise the more closely the ideas actually raised in the partner match the intended ones. The source of the passage is Ostwald's metaphor of language as a vehicle carrying a load of thought.
- **Concept before term in practice** (Wüster 1968, *The Machine Tool*, §2.17, via Savatovsky): "Each rubric originates, not from a term, but from a [concept] and the definition of that [concept] … it was determined which term best corresponded to the definition."
- **Definitions** (Savatovsky's characterisation, not a quotation): a Wüsterian definition is a real definition, an arrangement of characteristics. It admits functional relations as well as genus/species and part/whole relations.
- **Style of the 1979 book** (Budin's characterisation): an overview of Wüster's whole system written in a "hoch verdichtet" (highly compressed) style that assumes the reader knows his earlier work. Read today, the summary is itself a warning that compression offloads cost onto the reader.

## Relevance to AI answer style

Wüster gives the cleanest statement of the line between **precision** and **compression**:

- **Precision** means picking the one designation tied to a defined concept. The reader then rebuilds the intended concept with little "loss of cargo."
- **Compression** means saving effort ("Bequemlichkeit") at the cost of that match.

For an assistant this gives three operational principles:

1. Decide which concept is meant before choosing words.
2. Keep one term per concept throughout an answer. A synonym used for variety reads as a second concept, and the reader carries the memory cost of mapping it back.
3. Univocity is relative to context, so a term needs to be unambiguous only in this answer for this reader. Use a different designation only on purpose, to switch viewpoint (the *Kochsalz*/*Natriumchlorid* case), and say why.

Budin's remark that the 1979 book is "hoch verdichtet" is a direct case of compression failing: the book reads well only for readers who already hold the concept system.

## Candidate rules for the harness

1. **One concept, one term.** Once a technical term is chosen in an answer, reuse that exact term every time the concept recurs. Do not vary it for style. Introduce a second name only when it brings out a different aspect, and mark the switch ("also called X, which names it by its composition").
2. **Concept first, then name.** Before a key term's first use, check that it denotes exactly the intended concept for this reader. If it is ambiguous in this context, either replace it with the term that fits the definition or add a short definition by characteristics (genus plus distinguishing feature). Do not compress it into an undefined abbreviation.
3. **Minimise "loss of cargo," not words.** Judge a phrasing by how closely the idea the reader will form matches the intended one. Cut words only when this match is unchanged. If cutting forces the reader to supply concepts they may not have, the text is too dense, as with the "hoch verdichtet" 1979 book.
