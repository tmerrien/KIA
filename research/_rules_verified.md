### KEPT: [R1] Recoverability test for every cut: delete a word, clause or sentence only if this reader can rebuild its content from what they have actually seen in the conversation (their messages, visible turns, shown output) or from domain defaults. - Lens 1: survives; attribution WEAK. The counterexample does not refute the rule. The rule's own guard already covers all three of its cases. (1) Restating a destructive command matches "does not require a cut where a misreading is costly". The cited Shannon-1948 note (lesson 3) says this directly: repeat only what is costly to get wrong, "such as a destructive command", treating readback as channel coding. (2) Gibson-2019's "wordy or pedantic" is a warning against over-explicitness. The rule allows cuts but never forces restating, so the warning does not conflict with it. (3) A term the model saw 40 lines ago is the "reader's lossy memory, not the model's context" clause.

The core test does hold up against the notes. Piantadosi-2012 §2.1 says omission is efficient only for bits in H[M] but not in H[M|C]. It names "leaves out information other than what is provided in the context" as inefficient ambiguity, and it assumes a shared coding scheme; the note glosses this as the user's context, not the assistant's. Shannon-1951 says a reduced text is recoverable only for an "identical twin" predictor. Juba-2011 puts a price of about lg α bits on uncertainty about the listener's prior. Gibson-2019 defines efficiency as including success. "Never distinctions" has some support in Ferreira-2005, which reaches the rule only second-hand through the Piantadosi note: speakers resolve conceptual (referent) ambiguity but not purely lexical ambiguity.

Attribution is still overextended in three places:

Two real gaps remain, each a qualification rather than a refutation:
1. **Useful redundancy outside the misreading-cost case.** Degen et al. 2020 and Rubio-Fernandez 2016 show that a recoverable but more precise backup (an exact identifier added to a vague noun) speeds the listener up and lowers error, even when nothing is at stake. The guard only protects cuts "where a misreading is costly", so as worded the rule would allow cutting a useful exact identifier. The same literature also shows redundancy has a cost when it is surprising, which supports cutting vague redundancy.
2. **The test depends on a judgment writers make badly.** These sources come from my background knowledge, not the local notes:

   - McNamara, Kintsch, Songer & Kintsch 1996 (Cognition and Instruction 14(1)): low-knowledge readers are harmed by cohesion gaps, while high-knowledge readers benefit.
   - Kalyuga's expertise reversal effect.
   
   The phrase "this reader" handles this in principle, and McNamara actually supports gap-leaving for experts. But the rule gives no direction to lean when the reader's knowledge is uncertain.

Testing it on realistic answers turned up four real gaps. These call for revision, not refutation.

(1) Recovery cost. Piantadosi Sec. 2.2 requires that disambiguation be "not prohibitively costly", but the rule only asks whether the reader *can* rebuild the content. In a debugging answer, "apply the fix from earlier to the other handler" passes the test because the user can scroll up and reconstruct it. It still makes the answer worse than restating the 3-token change.

(2) "Domain defaults" is not tied to this reader. In a teaching answer, cutting an algebra step because it is a domain default for an expert fails a novice who lacks that default. In a medical answer, dropping "in adults" lets the reader fill in the adult default for a pediatric case. Scope qualifiers and hedges like these are not neatly "two candidate meanings", so the distinction clause may not catch them.

(3) Answers often travel beyond the conversation. They get pasted into tickets, Slack or a chart note. "What this reader has seen" licenses cutting referents that a forwarded reader never saw.

(4) Shannon 1948 and CAP 413 show redundancy being placed deliberately, not just left uncut. The rule is deletion-only, which is fine for a cut test but should not be read as the whole policy.

Applying the rule exposes three real defects. Each one calls for tighter wording rather than refuting the rule.

(1) Must-haves and K conflict, and the rule gives no order between them. Example: a user asks only for the adult dose of a drug (K = 1), but a renal-impairment contraindication is decision-critical. "Stopping at K" could cut the warning, while "keep every must-have" says to keep it. The must-have has to win, and K should only bound the optional units.

(2) The anchor mandate conflicts with the decision guard. Example: in a debugging answer, "fixed in v3.2.1, released 2023-04-05, in PR #812" keeps every which/when/where, but only the version number changes the user's action. Requiring anchors on every claim pads the answer, and wei-2024 A.7 shows that specific details also raise error rates.

(3) Treating the answer as a list of atomic facts misses procedural and teaching content. Nenkova says outright that SCUs ignore dependencies between units and their ordering. Song notes that advice, code and analogies are not verifiable claims. Example: a migration runbook can be shortened while keeping every fact yet lose "run the migration BEFORE deploying the new build". Under this rule that is a lossless cut, but in practice it breaks production. Mayer 1996 points the same way: a teaching summary works because it is a coordinated causal sequence, not a bag of facts.

Attribution has two overreaches. First, "the user's K" is presented as elicited from the user. In wei-2024, K is a per-dataset median chosen as a modeling assumption, not elicited per user, and wei's metric gives no credit past K but never says to stop at K. Song-2024 argues against this very use of K. Second, Nenkova's weights count how many reference summaries contain a unit; they do not measure decision impact. The verdict is a narrow keep: the rule survives with tighter wording. **Revised:** When you shorten an answer, first list the units the user needs. These are facts, conditions, warnings, and steps together with their order and dependencies. Then label each unit must-have or optional by whether it would change what the user decides or does, not by how many facts it holds. Every must-have stays, even if that takes you past the scope the question implies. Cut optional units lowest-impact first, and stop when the answer covers the scope the question asks for. Reword before you delete: use shorter sentences, the exact term, and no empty hedges. Keep only the anchors the user acts on or would check (which, when, where, how much), at the precision the evidence supports. Coarsen an uncertain anchor rather than making one up. - Lens 1: survives; attribution WEAK. The core of the rule holds up against the notes. ISO 704 5.4.4 says removing an essential characteristic changes the extension, and qualifiers that do not narrow the referent set can be cut. Seppala's necessary-condition test lists the same safe deletions: categorizers (G3.1.4), example lists replaced by a cover term (G9.4) and evaluative words (G9). The wording overreaches in three places:
(1) It names 'usually' as a qualifier never to delete. Two cited sources do the opposite. The absolute 'Never' also clashes with the rule's own guard, 'remove a hedge only after checking the unhedged claim is true'.
(2) 'Evaluative adjectives' are safe to delete only in Seppala's context, definitions ('delicious', 'beautiful'). The stated counterexamples do not break the rule's core, because each fits the truth-conditions guard. A negation that separates siblings is truth-conditional, so it is kept. A hedge that was only a tic can go once the bare claim is checked. A 'sort of' that disappears after a term is ratified (Brennan & Clark) leaves the extension unchanged. Skelton's critique is unverified. The rule survives with tighter wording. **Revised:** Never let trimming change the claim. The test for every cut is whether the claim's extension or truth conditions change. Delete freely only words that do not narrow the claim: categorizers ('a type of'), filler hedges and stance phrases that carry no real uncertainty, and example lists that one cover term fully replaces. Keep by default any qualifier that acts as a necessary condition (version, scope, platform, condition, exception), any approximator marking a rounded figure ('about', 'roughly'), and any frequency or modal hedge ('usually', 'may'). Remove such a hedge or qualifier only after checking that the unhedged claim is true. Evaluative words go only when they are not part of what is asserted. After any concision pass, check that modality, causality, negation and scope survive ('may help' must not become 'achieves'; a correlation must not become a cause). A negation that is itself the distinguishing feature stays.
- Lens 2: survives; attribution ok. Under the COUNTEREVIDENCE lens, I found nothing showing that keeping truth-conditional qualifiers harms correctness. The empirical work supports the rule. Kim et al. 2024 (FAccT, n=404) found that first-person hedges ("I'm not sure, but...") lowered user trust and agreement with the answer, but raised user accuracy by cutting overreliance on wrong answers. Degen et al. 2020 shows that a redundant but more precise modifier improves recovery of the meaning. Some work shows hedges can undermine persuasion: Durik et al. 2008 found this for data or colloquial hedges, and Erickson 1978 found more hedges lower witness credibility. That is a cost to persuasion, not to truth, and the rule's goal is to preserve the claim. Jensen 2008 even finds hedges raise scientists' trustworthiness.

The stated counterexamples mostly agree with the rule or are already covered by its guard:
- Seppala keeps a negation that separates two siblings, which is exactly what the rule says to preserve.
- Juba warns against adding qualifiers, not against keeping them.
- ISO 10241-1 deletes 'usually', but as a justified, recorded change of content. The guard allows exactly that: remove a hedge only after checking the unhedged claim is true.
- Pinker's "tic" hedges fall under "hedges that mark nothing".

Two real weaknesses remain:
